## 1. Nobody picks a career at seven

Let me start with a confession about the title. You are not going to pick a career for your child. Nobody can do that for a seven-year-old, and you should be wary of anyone who says they can.

What you can do is smaller, and it works better. You can notice. Then you can feed what you notice.

### The label that almost picked mine

When I was a kid, the grown-ups picked a path for me without meaning to. C's in math. ADHD. Could not sit still. Forgot things. Always in trouble. The verdict came in early: not a math kid.

If that label had stuck, my path was set. No numbers. Probably no college. Certainly no scholarship.

Here is what nobody noticed. When no one was making me, I trained. I trained my body, short sessions, every day, trying to beat yesterday. That was the real talent. It was never "sports" and it was never "math." It was the habit of training.

In high school I pointed that habit at my brain. Mental math and abacus drills, fifteen minutes a day. Around week three it clicked. Grades went up with no extra study. Then a perfect ACT. Then a full ride. Then Scholar-Athlete of the Year at a Division I school.

Same kid. Same ADHD. A different kind of smart.

### What this means for your child

Ability is built. It is not handed out at birth and sorted by second grade. That means two things for you.

First, any label your child has picked up is a guess, not a fact. "Not a math kid." "Not creative." "Shy." All guesses.

Second, your real job is to watch what your child does when nobody is making them. That is where the clues are. Then you feed it, a little, every day, the way you would feed a plant. Not with a flood. With a steady drip.

The rest of this guide shows you how.

## 2. The two-week watch

For two weeks, you are going to be a quiet scientist. You watch. You write it down. You do not comment.

### Why only watch

The moment you say "Oh, you love drawing!" your child starts drawing for you. Now you are watching a show, not a clue. So for two weeks, say nothing about what you see. Just write.

### What to watch for

Three questions. That is all.

- **What do they choose?** When the day is free, what do they reach for first? Not what they say they like. What their hands go to.
- **What do they repeat?** What do they do again and again, long after it is "done"? The same game, the same kind of build, the same kind of question.
- **What do they fix?** What makes them stop and try again on their own? A drawing they redo. A tower they rebuild. A word they look up. A friend they go back to after a fight. What a child fixes is what a child cares about.

### When to watch

The best clues come from free time. Weekend mornings. The hour after school. Car rides. The wait at the doctor. Any time nothing is planned and no screen is on.

Screens make the watch useless. A child on a tablet is choosing what the app chose for them. If you can, keep a few screen-free hours each week during the two weeks.

### What you will probably find

You will find more than one thing. Many kids show two or three patterns, not one. That is good news. It means more doors are open.

You may also find something that surprises you, or something you do not like. A child who argues all day. A child who takes things apart and does not put them back. Write it down anyway. Some of the best clues look like trouble at first. Mine did.

### Questions parents ask

**"I watched for two weeks and found nothing."** You found something. You just have not seen it yet. Look at what they fixed. That column is often the quiet one. If it is still blank, watch for two more weeks with fewer plans on the calendar. Bored kids show their hand.

**"The pattern changed after a month."** Good. Kids change. That is why you run the watch again every three months. Feed the new one. Keep a little of the old one going.

**"All my child wants is screens."** Then the watch starts with a screen-free hour. Expect a few days of complaining. Then they reach for something. What they reach for is your first clue.

**"Should I tell my child what I found?"** Not as a label. Show them what they did. "You built that bridge three times. What changed each time?" Let them tell you what they like.

## 3. Eight talent patterns, part one

Now look at your circled words. Find the patterns below that match. For each one, you get three things. What it looks like. How to feed it. And what not to do.

A word of warning. These are not boxes. They are lenses. Your child will fit more than one, and that is the point.

### Pattern 1: Space and shape

**What it looks like.** Your child draws maps. They do jigsaw puzzles without looking at the box. They remember the way to a place after one trip. They see how a thing will look when it is turned around.

**Feed it.** Puzzles that get harder. A sketchbook that is only theirs. Let them guide the way home from the park. Ask them to draw a map of their room from above. The abacus belongs here too, because the mental abacus is a picture your child learns to hold and move.

**Do not.** Do not call it "just doodling." Do not push neat handwriting so hard that they stop drawing.

**Where people use it.** Design and building. Medicine. Maps and flight. Art. Any job where you see a thing in your head before it exists.

### Pattern 2: Words

**What it looks like.** Your child reads past bedtime with a flashlight. They make up words. They argue, and they are good at it. They retell a movie scene by scene. They notice when you use a word wrong.

**Feed it.** Read aloud together, even after they can read alone. Let them read you something too. Give them a notebook for stories. Play word games at dinner. When they argue, ask for their best reason and listen to all of it.

**Do not.** Do not punish the arguing. Shape it. "Give me your best reason, calmly" teaches more than "Don't talk back."

**Where people use it.** Law. Writing. Teaching. Sales. Any job where you have to make someone see what you see.

### Pattern 3: People

**What it looks like.** Your child knows who is upset before anyone says a word. They set the rules for the playground game. They make friends in a new place in ten minutes. Younger kids follow them around.

**Feed it.** Give them real jobs with people. Let them order for the family at a restaurant. Let them plan a sibling's birthday game. Ask "how do you think she felt?" after a story.

**Do not.** Do not treat social skill as a distraction from "real" school. It is real. Do not let it turn into bossing. "Did everyone get a turn?" keeps a leader honest.

**Where people use it.** Running a team. Medicine. Teaching. Any business. Most of the hardest jobs are hard because of people.

### Pattern 4: Building

**What it looks like.** Your child takes things apart. They build forts out of everything. They want to know how the toilet works. They use tape on things that do not need tape.

**Feed it.** A box of real tools and scrap wood, with you nearby. Old electronics to take apart (unplugged, batteries out). Building sets with no picture on the box. Ask "how would you make it stronger?"

**Do not.** Do not save every build. Let them knock it down and start over. Rebuilding is where they learn. Do not hand them kits that only go together one way, every time.

**Where people use it.** Engineering. Trades. Surgery. Making anything. The world runs on people who can build and fix.

## 4. Eight talent patterns, part two

### Pattern 5: Movement

**What it looks like.** Your child cannot sit still. They climb everything. They learn a new move by watching it once. They do cartwheels in the kitchen.

This was me. And here is what I want you to hear. The kid who cannot sit still is not a problem to manage. It is an engine to point.

**Feed it.** Daily time to move hard, outside if you can. A sport or dance where they can train and get measurably better. And let them stand or bounce at the table when they work. The abacus is math you do with your hands, so a moving kid often likes it more than a worksheet.

**Do not.** Do not treat movement as the reward for "real" work. It is real work. Do not let the label "can't focus" become the story. Mine almost did.

**Where people use it.** Sport and coaching. Medicine. The trades. Performing. And, like me, the habit of training carries into everything else.

### Pattern 6: Music and rhythm

**What it looks like.** Your child hums all day. They tap beats on the table. They remember songs after one listen. They notice when something is out of tune.

**Feed it.** A real instrument, even a cheap one. Ten minutes of practice a day beats an hour on Saturday, the same rule as everything else. Sing together in the car. The number game at dinner works well for this child, because the numbers come in a beat.

**Do not.** Do not turn it into a chore with a chart and a fight every night. Keep the practice short. Let them play what they want for part of it.

**Where people use it.** Music. Sound and film. Teaching. And the habit of daily practice goes anywhere.

### Pattern 7: Numbers

**What it looks like.** Your child counts things. Stairs, cars, cereal pieces. They ask how much things cost. They keep score in every game. They like to know who is winning and by how much.

**Feed it.** Let them count the change. Let them work out the tip. Ask "how many minutes until we get there?" Play the number game at dinner. Give them the abacus. This is the child the mental abacus was made for, and the ten stages give them a climb that school will not.

**Do not.** Do not let them be bored. A number kid who is bored in math class can decide they hate math. Do not race them through school math either. Depth beats speed.

**Where people use it.** Every field. That is what chapter 6 is about.

### Pattern 8: Care

**What it looks like.** Your child looks after the pet without being told. They notice the kid sitting alone. They want to help the nurse at the doctor. They keep a plant alive.

**Feed it.** Give them something real to care for. A pet, a plant, a younger cousin to read to. Let them help cook for someone who is sick. Ask them what they think a person needs.

**Do not.** Do not tell them it is "sweet" and move on. Care is a skill, and it can be trained like any other. Do not let them carry grown-up worries, either. Care for a plant, yes. Care for a parent's stress, no.

**Where people use it.** Medicine. Teaching. Science that helps people. Any job where someone's life is better because you showed up.

### Now pick two

Look at your worksheet again. Pick the two patterns that fit best. Write them in the plan in chapter 8. You will feed those two first, for three months. Then you watch again.

## 5. The mistakes that kill a talent

Talents are easy to spot and easy to kill. Often the damage comes from kind parents who mean well. Here are the ones that, to my mind, do the most damage.

### Naming it too soon

"She's my little artist." It sounds sweet. But a child who has been named starts to protect the name. They stop trying new things, in case they are bad at them. Describe what they did, not who they are. "You drew that bridge three times until it looked right" beats "You're an artist."

### Pouring it on

You spot a talent and sign up for three classes, a summer camp and a private coach. Within a year the child hates it. A talent needs a steady drip, not a flood. Fifteen minutes a day of real practice beats a packed calendar.

### Dropping the base

Some parents find a talent and let the rest go. "He's a builder, he doesn't need to read much." Every talent on the list gets bigger with strong reading and strong numbers. Those two are the floor every other talent stands on.

### Only feeding what you like

A parent who loves music sees the music. A parent who loves sport sees the sport. Your two-week worksheet is there to stop you from seeing only yourself. Trust the sheet.

### Fixing it for them

When a child is stuck, the urge is to step in. Don't. The fixing is where the growth is. Ask one question, then wait. In the Playbook, chapter 8 has the exact words for the abacus. They work for everything else, too.

### Believing the label

This is the big one. "Not a math kid." "Not sporty." "Not a reader." Every label is a guess about where your child is today. None of them is a fact about where they can go. I am the proof I needed.

## 6. The skill that goes with them anywhere

Your child might be a builder or a talker. They might end up in a lab or on a stage. Whatever they pick, there is one skill I would give every child first. It is the ability to work with numbers in their head, fast and right.

### Why this one

Numbers show up in every field. The builder measures. The doctor works out a dose. The writer runs a budget. The coach reads the stats. A person who can hold numbers in their head thinks faster in every one of those rooms.

And most kids are not getting it at school. In 2024, only 28% of American eighth graders were proficient in math. Scores were back to where they were twenty-five years ago [1].

I want to be straight with you here. No study has followed abacus kids into their careers. What follows is what the research does show, and then my own case.

### What the research shows

- **Better arithmetic.** In a three-year trial in classrooms in India, kids who trained on the mental abacus about three hours a week beat kids who got the same time in extra math. They were better at arithmetic and calculation. They also understood place value better [2].
- **A steadier picture.** A five-year study of 144 children found the abacus group got better at holding pictures in mind. Their brain scans changed too [3].
- **More than one study.** A 2021 review looked across five studies and found gains in arithmetic [4].
- **It is a picture, not words.** Children who do mental abacus can do it while talking. The beads are a picture in the head, not a string of words. Most kids hold about three or four columns at once [5].

Neither the trial nor the five-year study found a jump in IQ. I will not tell you otherwise. What they found is a child who calculates better, with a steadier picture in their head.*

### My case

Here is how I think about it. Most school math is memory. Memory runs out. Somewhere around algebra, kids who memorized hit a wall. The ones who can move numbers in their head keep going.

That is what happened to me. Same kid, same ADHD. I trained the picture fifteen minutes a day, and the rest of school got easier with no extra study.

### How it fits each pattern

- The **space** child already thinks in pictures. The abacus gives the picture numbers.
- The **word** child gets numbers they can say out loud, move by move.
- The **people** child gets a game they can play with the family at dinner.
- The **builder** gets a tool with rules they can master with their hands.
- The **mover** gets math they can do standing up, with busy hands.
- The **music** child gets numbers in a beat.
- The **number** child gets a ladder with ten stages to climb.
- The **carer** gets something to teach a younger sibling.

## 7. The next ten years

A seven-year-old today will finish college in about fifteen years. Nobody can tell you what the job market will look like then. Be careful of anyone who says they can.

What we can do is look at what employers say they want now, and what has stayed true for a long time.

### What employers say now

Every few years, the World Economic Forum asks more than a thousand employers which skills matter most. In its 2025 report, the top core skill was analytical thinking. About seven in ten employers called it essential. It held the top spot in past reports too [6]. Skills with AI and big data were among those rising fastest [6].

Analytical thinking means taking a problem apart and working it through. That is a trained skill. It is the same thing your child does on the abacus every day: look at the problem, decide the move, make it, check it.

### What AI changes

AI tools can now do some of the work that new hires used to do. What that will mean for any one job in ten years, nobody knows.

Here is what I think a parent can count on. A person who can think a problem through will still be useful. So will a person who can check whether an answer makes sense. A kid who can add numbers in their head knows at a glance when the screen is wrong. That kid is in charge of the tool, not the other way around.

### What has not changed

Some things have been true for a long time and show no sign of stopping.

- People who can learn new things quickly keep finding work.
- People who can work with others are hard to replace.
- People with a training habit get better at whatever they point it at.

That last one is the one I lived. The habit of training was worth more to me than any one subject. Fifteen minutes a day, measured, trying to beat yesterday. That habit travels into any field your child picks.

### What to do with this

You do not need to guess the future. You need a child who can think. A child who can learn new things and keep going when it gets hard. Feed the talent you saw in your two-week watch. Build the number skill underneath it. Protect the daily habit. Then let your child pick the career when they are old enough to pick it.
