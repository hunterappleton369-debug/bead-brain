### How to use this guide

Each chapter is one habit. Each habit has four parts. First, the habit itself. Then why it works, in two short sections. Then the exact words to say. Last, a five-minute version for the busy nights.

Do not start all eight habits on Monday. Pick one. Run it for two weeks. Then add the next. A habit you keep beats three you drop.

You will see small numbers in brackets, like [1]. Those point to the sources at the back. Where there is no number, I am telling you what I do and what I have seen. I will always tell you which is which.

## 1. The kid nobody bet on

I was not the kid who was going to be on stage at graduation.

I got C's in math. I had ADHD. I could not sit still, I forgot things, and I was always in trouble. Somewhere along the way the grown-ups decided I was "not a math kid." They were kind about it. That is why it stuck.

### The wall

In high school I wanted to play college sports. The coaches liked me. My grades did not. My GPA was a wall, and I could not talk my way over it. I needed a test score so big that no one could argue with it.

So I did the one thing I knew how to do. I trained.

### What I tried

I already knew how to train my body. Short sessions. Every day. One thing at a time. Measure it, then beat it. I had never pointed that at my brain. Now I did.

Mental math and abacus drills, fifteen minutes a day, the way I trained my body. There was no kit. There was no parent book. There was no trainer app. I pieced it together alone and guessed what came next.

### The click

The first two weeks felt like nothing. Then, around week three, it clicked. I started to like it.

My grades went up with no extra study. Then the ACT came back perfect. Then a full ride. Then Scholar-Athlete of the Year at a Division I school.

Same kid. Same ADHD. A different kind of smart.

### What I learned about parents

Here is the part this guide is about.

Nobody hovered over me. No one sat on my homework every night. What changed me was a small habit, done every day, that taught me to think. Hovering builds a kid who waits for help. Training builds a kid who can think.

So I asked myself a question. What would the right parent have done for me, from week one of school? Not a louder parent. Not a parent who checked every page. A calm one, who built the habits and then got out of the way.

The answer is a short list. I call the parents who run it the top 1%. Nobody measured them. I use the name because so few parents do all of these things at once. You can do every one of them. None of them costs money. Most take less than fifteen minutes.

The next eight chapters are those habits.

## 2. Fifteen minutes beats two hours

**The habit:** practice a little every day, instead of a lot once a week.

A lot of families do learning the way they do laundry. It piles up all week. Then on Saturday, there is a big push. Two hours at the table. A packet of sheets. A tired kid and a tired parent.

The top 1% do it the other way. Fifteen minutes. Every day. Same chair.

### Why it works

The brain keeps what it uses often. When practice is spread out over many days, kids remember more of it later. Researchers call this the spacing effect. In one German study, third and seventh graders did the same math practice two ways. One group did it all in one day. The other group spread it over three days. The spread-out group did better on later tests, most clearly in seventh grade [1]. In a study in England, seventh graders who spread practice across three weeks beat a group who crammed it into one session, a month later [2].

There is a second reason, and it is the one I lived. A short session ends before your child is tired. So it ends on a win. A kid who ends on a win wants to come back tomorrow. Two hours on Saturday ends in tears more often than not. Then Saturday becomes the day everyone dreads. My training worked because it was short enough that I never had a reason to skip it.

### The words to say

At the start: "Fifteen minutes. Then you're free."

When the timer goes: "Done. Good work. Same time tomorrow."

If they beg to keep going: "Let's stop while it's fun. You can have more tomorrow."

That last one feels wrong the first time you say it. Say it anyway. You want them to leave hungry.

### The five-minute version

Set a timer for five minutes. Do one thing. Read ten numbers off the abacus, or run the day's quest in the trainer. Stop when the timer stops. Five minutes every day still beats an hour on Sunday.

For how the full fifteen minutes runs, see Playbook chapter 2.

## 3. Praise the move, not the kid

**The habit:** when your child gets something right, name what they did. Do not name what they are.

You know the moment. Your child solves a hard one. Your heart jumps. And out it comes: "You're so smart!"

It feels like the kindest thing you can say. The top 1% bite their tongue and say something else.

### Why it works

"You're so smart" tells a child they have a fixed amount of something. So the next hard problem feels like a test of that thing. If they miss, maybe they are not smart after all. Kids who hear this a lot start to avoid hard problems. Hard problems are where the growth is.

A study at Temple University filmed parents talking with their toddlers at home. Then it checked on the kids five years later. The parents who praised effort, plans and actions had kids who liked a challenge more and believed they could get better with work. Praise for traits, like "you're so smart," did not show that link [3]. So name the move. A child who is praised for the move repeats the move.

### The words to say

Instead of "You're so smart," try one of these:

- "I heard you say 'plus five, take away four.' That was it."
- "You checked it and fixed it yourself."
- "You went left to right the whole way."
- "That one was hard, and you stayed with it."
- "You were slower today, and you got every one right. That's the deal."

Notice that each one points to something your child can do again tomorrow.

### The five-minute version

Tonight, catch one move. Just one. Say it out loud, plain and specific. Then stop talking. One true sentence beats a speech.

## 4. The slot on the family calendar

**The habit:** practice has a fixed time and a fixed place, written down, like a game or a lesson you paid for.

Here is a Tuesday in a lot of houses. Dinner runs late. Someone has a project due. The dog gets sick. At eight o'clock a parent remembers practice. The child is already in pajamas. "We'll do double tomorrow." Tomorrow never comes.

The top 1% do not decide each day whether practice happens. The calendar decided weeks ago.

### Why it works

When something has a set time, nobody has to argue about it. "Is today a bead day?" is a question your child can ask a hundred times. "It's 7:15, so it's bead time" is not a question. It is just what happens at 7:15.

Anyone who has trained for a sport knows this. Practice is at a set time. You do not decide to go. You go, because that is what that hour is for. I trained my brain the same way I trained my body, fifteen minutes a day. A set time does the work that willpower cannot.

### The words to say

When you set it up: "Bead time is right after breakfast. Every day but Sunday. It's on the calendar, just like your other practice."

When your child pushes back: "It's 7:15. That's bead time. Fifteen minutes, then you're free."

When life blows up the slot: "We missed our time. Let's do five minutes now so the streak lives."

### Pick the slot

Good slots are attached to something that already happens every day.

- Right after breakfast, before shoes.
- Right after the school snack.
- Right before the bath.

Bad slots float. "Sometime after dinner" is not a slot. Neither is "when we get a chance."

Write it on the paper calendar on the fridge, in pen. Put it in your phone with an alarm. If your child is old enough, let them write it in. A kid who wrote the slot owns the slot.

### The five-minute version

Tonight, pick the slot. Write it down. Tell your child the time. That is the whole job tonight.

## 5. Read aloud, then read numbers aloud

**The habit:** read to your child, and read numbers to your child, at the table, out loud.

A lot of parents stop reading aloud once their child can read alone. The top 1% keep going. And they add a strange little game at dinner. They read numbers out loud, and the child adds them in their head.

### Why it works

Words heard add up. A team at Ohio State counted the words in sixty popular picture books. A child read one book a day hears about 290,000 more words by age five than a child who is not read to [4]. That study looked at young children. Your seven- or nine-year-old is past that age, and that is fine. Reading aloud still gives them words that are harder than the ones they can read alone. It also gives you ten quiet minutes side by side.

Numbers read aloud work the same way. Every proven abacus school in the world uses dictation. The teacher reads numbers in a steady beat. The child keeps pace on the beads, then in their head. There is a reason it has lasted. Your child cannot look back at a number you said. They have to hold it. Holding it is the skill. Playbook chapter 6 has the full dinner-table game.

### The words to say

For the story: "One chapter tonight. You pick the book."

For the numbers: "Ready? Three. Plus four. Plus two. What's the total?"

When they get it: "You held all three. Want a longer one?"

When they miss: "Let's do it again, slower." Then say it again, slower. Do not give the answer.

### How to run the number game

Start small. Two or three single numbers. Say them in a steady beat, about one a second. Wait for the answer.

When your child gets five in a row, add one more number. When that is easy, try two-digit numbers. Keep it short. Five rounds is plenty. End while they want more.

Let siblings take turns being the reader. A younger child who reads numbers out loud is practicing too.

### The five-minute version

At dinner, three rounds of the number game. That is it. Read the story another night.

## 6. Sleep first, screens last

**The habit:** guard sleep like it is part of the lesson. Screens go off an hour before bed and stay out of the bedroom.

This is the habit nobody wants to hear about. It sounds like a health class. It is not. It is the cheapest learning tool you have.

### Why it works

A panel of sleep doctors set the target for kids aged 6 to 12: nine to twelve hours of sleep a night, on a regular basis. The American Academy of Pediatrics backed it. The panel tied regular sleep in that range to better learning, mood and behavior [5]. A short night shows up at the table the next day.

Screens are the usual thief. The American Academy of Pediatrics says to stop screens about an hour before bed. It also says to keep phones and screens out of a child's bedroom at night [6]. A phone in the bedroom is a phone that gets checked at ten o'clock. That is not a willpower problem. It is a room problem.

### The words to say

At the start: "New house rule. Screens sleep in the kitchen. They charge there all night."

At the one-hour mark: "Screens off. Pick a book or a game with me."

When they argue: "It's not a punishment. Your brain does its best work while you sleep. We're giving it the time."

And the line that works best of all is the one you live. Put your own phone in the kitchen too.

### Set the slot for sleep

Work back from wake-up time. If school means a 6:45 wake-up, nine and a half hours puts lights out near 9:15. Screens off near 8:15. Bead time, if it is in the evening, goes before that, not after.

Never run a Bead Score test on a tired night. A tired kid scores two rungs under where they live. Playbook chapter 7 has more on when to test.

### The five-minute version

Tonight, pick the charging spot outside the bedroom. Plug in every screen in the house there, yours too, one hour before bed.

## 7. Talk to the teacher like a teammate

**The habit:** make the teacher your partner in week one, before there is a problem.

Many parents meet the teacher twice a year. Once at the open house. Once at the conference, when there is already a problem. By then the teacher has a picture of your child, and it is hard to change.

The top 1% send one short note in week one. They ask one good question. Then they mostly stay out of the way.

### Why it works

A teacher has twenty or more kids. Most of the notes they get are complaints. A note that says "I want to help at home, tell me one thing" is rare. It tells the teacher you are on their side. When something comes up later, you are already on the same team.

It also gets you the most useful thing a teacher knows: what your child does when they get stuck in class. You cannot see that from home. The teacher sees it every day. That one answer tells you what to work on at the table.

I was the kid who was always in trouble. I wish someone had asked my teachers two things. What did I do well? And what did I do when I got stuck? Those are the questions I would ask now.

### The words to say

The week-one note, by email or on paper:

"Hi, I'm Sam's mom. Thank you for this year. We practice a little mental math at home every day, about fifteen minutes. Is there one thing you'd like us to work on with Sam this month? Happy to help however is useful."

At the conference, three questions, one at a time:

- "What does she do when she gets stuck?"
- "When is she at her best in class?"
- "What is one thing we can do at home?"

What not to say:

- Do not ask for more homework.
- Do not compare your child to another child.
- Do not argue a grade in the first meeting. Ask what the grade means first.

### The five-minute version

Write the week-one note tonight. Four sentences. Send it tomorrow morning.

## 8. Hands in your lap

**The habit:** when your child is stuck, ask one question and wait. Never give the answer.

Your child is at the beads. A rod shows four. The next step is plus one. There is no free bead. They stare. The seconds stretch. Every part of you wants to say "bring the five down."

The top 1% sit on their hands. Sometimes for real.

### Why it works

The choice is the skill. Every time your child makes the choice, the move gets a little more automatic. Every time you make it for them, they lose that rep. Worse, they learn a new move: wait for Mom.

A child who hears the answer learns that "stuck" means someone else will fix it. A child who is asked a question learns that "stuck" means look again. Five years from now, the second child sits down to a test they have never seen and starts. That is the kid who ends up on the stage.

### The words to say

The one question, every time: "Free beads, or friend?"

Then wait. Longer than feels okay. Count to ten in your head.

If they are still stuck: "Look at the rod. What does it show?"

If they rush: "Slow is fine."

If they want to quit: "We'll stop for today. Same time tomorrow." Then put the abacus away without a sigh.

Playbook chapter 8 has the full list of stuck moments and what to say in each one. Keep it near the table. The trainer's coach also prints one line for you at each stage. For the first stage it says: say every number out loud with your child, and stop while it is still fun.

### The five-minute version

For the next five minutes of practice, keep your hands in your lap. Literally. Notice how often they want to move. That urge is the habit you are breaking.

## 9. One number on the fridge

**The habit:** track one number. Put it where your child can see it. Watch it climb.

Report cards come three or four times a year. They are late, they are vague, and your child does not read them. "Meets expectations" is not a number a kid can chase.

The top 1% find one honest number and put it on the fridge. In our program, that number is the Bead Score.

### Why it works

A number a child can see is a number a child will chase. When I trained my body, I measured it and tried to beat it. A letter grade in math never gave me that. When I pointed the training at my brain, I measured that too, and the number gave me a reason to come back the next day.

The Bead Score is a ladder with twenty rungs. Your child climbs it in their head, no abacus and no paper, until one rung does not go. The top rung they clear is the score. The trainer charts it next to where kids the same age usually sit. It measures one thing, the picture of the beads in your child's head, and nothing else. That is what makes it honest.

### The words to say

After a test: "Rung five. Write it on the fridge in your own writing."

Before the next one: "Last time was five. Let's see where you are now."

If it drops: "Tired days happen. We'll do two normal weeks and try again."

To the grandparents, with your child in the room: "She's on rung eight. That's five two-digit numbers added in her head. Try it."

### How often

Every two to four weeks. Never on a tired night. Not more often, or the test turns into something your child dreads. Between tests, keep the daily practice the same size. Plateaus last about two weeks, then break. Playbook chapter 7 has the full ladder.

The first Bead Score test comes at the end of the fifth stage. Before that, the number on the fridge can be the day's best time on a drill. Same idea. One number, in your child's writing, getting better. The trainer's coach says it best for that stage: kids love watching a number fall.

### The five-minute version

Tonight, tape a blank sheet to the fridge. Write today's date and today's best drill time. Tomorrow, add another line.

## 10. The belief under all of it

Every habit in this guide rests on one belief. If you take one thing from these pages, take this.

**Intelligence is built, not born. And the mental abacus is the fastest way I know to build it at home, in fifteen minutes a day.**

### Why I believe it

I was the proof I needed. Same kid, same ADHD, a different kind of smart. Nothing about me changed except the training.

You do not have to take my word for it. In a three-year classroom trial in India, kids who trained on the mental abacus beat kids who got extra regular math on arithmetic and place value [7]. A five-year study of 144 children found the abacus group got better at holding pictures in mind, and their brain scans changed [8]. Neither study found a jump in IQ, and I will not tell you otherwise. What they found is a child who calculates faster and more accurately. That shows up in every test your child will ever sit.*

### How the habits fit together

Look back at the eight habits. Each one protects the same thing: fifteen good minutes a day.

- Short and daily is the dose.
- The calendar slot makes sure it happens.
- Praise for the move keeps your child coming back.
- Hands in your lap keeps the thinking theirs.
- Read-aloud and the number game add reps at the table.
- Sleep lets the day's work stick.
- The teacher tells you where to aim.
- The Bead Score shows the climb.

None of it is hovering. All of it is training.

### Your first week

Here is a plan for the next seven days. Do it in order.

- **Day 1.** Pick the slot. Write it on the fridge (chapter 4).
- **Day 2.** First session in the trainer. Stage 1, lesson one. Fifteen minutes, then stop (chapter 2).
- **Day 3.** Same slot. Catch one move and name it (chapter 3).
- **Day 4.** Move every screen to the kitchen at night (chapter 6).
- **Day 5.** Send the teacher your week-one note (chapter 7).
- **Day 6.** Three rounds of the number game at dinner (chapter 5).
- **Day 7.** Rest. Tape the blank sheet to the fridge for next week (chapter 9).

### Questions parents ask

**"My child already says they hate math."** Good. You found out early. Do not argue with it. Do not call it math. Call it bead time, keep it to ten minutes, and praise one move a day. The label usually stops sounding true around week three. That is when it happened for me.

**"I'm not a math person myself."** Neither was I, for a long time. You do not teach anything. The trainer picks every problem and checks every answer. Your job is the chair beside your child, one question, and your hands in your lap.

**"We only have weekends."** Then do five minutes on weekdays and fifteen on weekends. Five minutes a day still beats an hour once a week. Look for the slot that is already there, like the drive to school for the number game.

**"I have two kids, three years apart."** One slot, two chairs, one after the other. The older one reads numbers out loud for the younger one. The younger one times the older one. Each one gets their own number on the fridge.

**"What if we miss a week?"** Nothing is lost. Go back one lesson, run its drill until it passes, and carry on. Do not do double days to catch up. Just get back in the slot.

### Start tonight

Stage 1 is open in the trainer right now. Open it at childphenoms.com, make your child's profile, and run the first lesson in your new slot. It is called "Hold it, clear it, move it." It takes about ten minutes. You do not need the wooden abacus yet. The screen abacus makes the real one feel familiar the moment the box lands.

Phenom University takes it from there, one stage at a time, with every lesson picked and graded for you. Your job stays the same the whole way. A chair. A question. Fifteen minutes.
