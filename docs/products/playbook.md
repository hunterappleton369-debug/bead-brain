## Chapter 1: The lie that cost me a decade

Before you open the abacus, I need ten minutes. What I am about to tell you would have saved my parents ten years.

I was a terrible student. C's in math. ADHD, back when the plan for a kid like that was mostly to tell him to stop. I forgot everything. I could not sit still. I was always in trouble. The system had one answer for me: try harder, sit stiller, memorize more. I did none of those things well, so the verdict came in early. Not a math kid. Not a school kid. Probably not a college kid.

Nobody said it cruelly. The teachers were kind about it, and that is what made it stick. A label handed to you kindly at seven is a label you are still carrying at seventeen.

Then, in high school, I got an idea that would not leave me alone. I wanted to play college sports. The coaches liked me. The admissions office did not. My grades were a wall, and no amount of wanting was going to move it. The only way over was a standardized test score so big that nobody could argue with it.

So I did something strange for a bad student. I stopped studying harder and started training differently.

I knew how to train. I had been training my body for years: short sessions, every day, one thing at a time, measure it, beat it. I had never once pointed that at my brain. Now I did. Mental math. Abacus drills. Fifteen minutes a day, every day, the way I ran sprints.

The first two weeks were nothing. Beads, numbers, slow. I felt stupid holding the thing. Then something changed around week three.

I started liking it.

Not the subject. The feeling of my brain getting faster. Numbers stopped being symbols to memorize and became things I could move. I could feel a sum settle the way you feel a ball land in your hands. Tests stopped scaring me, because I could think faster than the test. My grades went up without extra studying. Then the ACT came back perfect. Then the full ride. Then Scholar-Athlete of the Year at a Division I school.

Same kid. Same ADHD. A different kind of smart.

Here is the part that still bothers me. Nothing about me changed except the training. The fidgeting that got me in trouble in class was the thing that made the beads work, because this is math you do with your hands. The brain that could not hold a lecture could hold a picture. For ten years I had been told I was missing something. I was not missing anything. I had been handed the wrong tool.

Your child is seven, or nine, or eleven. The window I found at seventeen is wide open for them right now. This book is the training I wish someone had handed my parents, written so you can run it at the kitchen table without being a math person. I was not one either.

### What this book is

It is the manual for the abacus in the box. Read it in an evening. Then run it, fifteen minutes a day, with the online trainer picking every problem and this book telling you what to say.

Chapter 2 is the routine. Chapter 3 is the lie, and how to break it in three weeks. Chapter 4 is every bead move your child will ever learn, all thirty-four of them, in order. Chapter 5 is the ten-stage road from reading beads to competition speed. Chapter 6 is the game that makes grandparents put their forks down. Chapter 7 is the one number that tells you where your child stands. Chapter 8 is what to say when they get stuck, which is the chapter you will come back to.

### What this book is not

It is not a math textbook, and you do not need to be good at math to run it. The trainer picks every problem, grades every answer, and shows the beads doing it when your child misses. You never prepare a lesson and you never mark a worksheet.

It is not a race. Some children read the beads in three days and some take three weeks, and both end up in the same place.

And it is not a program you supervise from across the room. The chair beside your child is the method. Fifteen minutes in that chair is worth more than an hour with a tutor, and chapter 2 is about why.

Three things will happen. In the first week your child will read numbers off the beads. In a few months the beads will come off the table and the math will go on inside their head. And the whole way, you will have a number that shows the climb.

Let's go.

## Chapter 2: Beads to brain: the fifteen-minute routine

The centers charge a hundred and fifty to two hundred and fifty dollars a month. For that, your child drives across town twice a week, sits in a room for two hours, and watches a teacher for most of it. Then comes home with a packet of worksheets.

Here is what you are going to do instead. Fifteen minutes. Six days a week. A chair next to your child. The beads never leave the table, and every one of those minutes is spent on them.

That is not a smaller version of what the centers do. It is a better one, and this chapter is about why.

### Why shorter wins

The thing your child is building is a picture: an abacus inside the head, with beads that move. Nobody builds that picture in a two-hour sitting. It grows one column at a time, the way a muscle grows, from a little load every day with sleep in between. A short session the brain can hold all the way through beats a long one it checks out of halfway.

The second reason is the hands. The beads are a motor skill, like a free throw. Motor skills are learned by doing the move, not by hearing about it. In a class of twelve, your child might touch the beads for fifteen minutes out of the two hours. At your table, they touch them for fifteen minutes out of fifteen.

And the third reason is you. A teacher at the front of a room cannot see the exact moment a child hesitates on a column. You can. The whole method of this book is built around that moment, and what you say in it.

Daily beats weekly. Short beats long. A few days off and the picture goes dull, so protect the streak. That is the entire schedule.

### What you need

Three things, and two of them are in the box.

The abacus. A thirteen-rod soroban: one bead above the beam, four below. It is not a toy, and it is not the ten-bead school abacus or the Chinese one with two beads on top. The tricks in chapter 4 only work on a soroban.

The trainer. Stage 1 of the online program is unlocked with your kit. It runs on any tablet or laptop. The screen abacus is for the demos and the first lessons; for real problems your child uses the wooden one, until the stage where most of the work moves into the head.

Fifteen minutes. Pick a spot and a time. The kitchen table after breakfast works. What matters is that it is the same spot and the same time, so nobody has to decide whether today is a bead day. Today is always a bead day.

### When to start

Most children start between five and seven, and the kit is built for seven to eleven. Before the first lesson your child should be able to count to a hundred, read a two-digit number, and tell you which of two numbers is bigger. If "tens and ones" means nothing yet, spend a week on that with coins or blocks first.

Under six, expect the first two stages to take twice as long, and keep sessions to ten minutes. If reading the beads has not clicked after a month, put the abacus away for a few months and come back. Nothing is lost.

### The fifteen minutes

Four parts, always in this order.

**Warm-up, two minutes.** Read ten numbers off the beads, or do the first item of the day's quest in the trainer. Eyes on the beads before anything hard happens.

**Lesson or drill, eight to ten minutes.** One lesson a day at most. Read the short explanation together. Press play on the demos. Then the drill: ten problems, chosen so they only need moves your child already knows. Nine out of ten right and the lesson is done. Not yet? Same drill tomorrow. Nobody moves on until it sticks.

**Mental, five minutes.** From the second stage on, every session ends with the soroban pushed away and a few problems done in the head, or a flash round. This is the part that builds the picture. It is the part parents skip to squeeze in more bead work, and it is the one part you must never skip.

**Log it, one minute.** Say the day's best time out loud. Every two to four weeks, take the Bead Score test instead of the mental drill, and write the number down.

### The hand

Two fingers touch the beads. The thumb pushes the bottom beads up to the beam. The index finger pushes them down, and moves the top bead both ways. That is the whole grip, and sloppy fingers now become slow fingers later, so hold your child to it from day one.

Work left to right. Hundreds, then tens, then ones. It is the opposite of what school teaches, and it is the reason mental math becomes possible: your child adds in the same order they say the number.

Say the move out loud. "Plus seven: take away three, carry one." The words fall away on their own once the hand knows the way.

Finish the problem before checking it. A child who restarts halfway through learns to panic. A child who finishes, checks, and redoes learns to trust the process.

Accuracy before speed. Nine out of ten slow beats seven out of ten fast, every time. Speed comes on its own.

### Your job during a drill

Sit beside your child, not across from them. You are looking at the same beads, from the same side.

When they hesitate, ask one question. "Free beads, or friend?" Then wait. Do not touch the beads. Do not say the answer. The decision is the skill, and every time you make it for them, you take a repetition away.

When they get it right, praise the formula they said out loud, not the answer. "I heard you say take away three, carry one. That was it." A child who is praised for the move repeats the move.

That is the whole job. A chair, a question, and patience.

## Chapter 3: The most expensive lie in education, and the three-week fix

"Not a math kid."

Somebody will say it about your child. A teacher, a relative, another parent. Maybe your child will say it first. It sounds like an observation. It is a sentence, in both senses of the word.

It cost me a decade. In this chapter I want to show you where the lie comes from, why it feels true, and the three weeks that break it.

### Where the lie comes from

School cannot make your child special. It is not built to. Every child in the room gets the same page, at the same pace, graded by the same test. Private school buys smaller classes and nicer buildings. It cannot buy a different lesson.

And the lesson rewards one thing: memorizing fast. Times tables, procedures, the steps to carry a one. The child who memorizes fastest looks like the math kid. The child who does not looks like the opposite. Nobody is measuring whether a child can think with numbers, because nobody in the room is teaching it.

That recipe is producing worse results every year. Twenty-eight percent of American eighth graders are proficient in math. Eighth-grade scores are back where they were twenty-five years ago. The room is not going to do this for your child.

Now look at who wins afterward. The founders, the surgeons, the kids with the scholarship. They do not out-memorize the room. They out-think it. They compute. That is a skill, and the reason nobody teaches it is that it cannot be taught from a worksheet.

### The memorizer's ceiling

There is a second cost, and it lands later. The child who was the math kid in third grade because they memorized fastest hits a wall in algebra, when memory runs out and the only thing left is thinking. Parents of those children are blindsided. The child who had always been good is suddenly not, and nobody can say why.

The abacus child does not hit that wall, because they were never memorizing. They were moving numbers. By the time algebra arrives, numbers have been things you can pick up and move for five years, and a letter standing in for one is not frightening. The skill that looked like a party trick at eight is the skill that holds at fourteen.

### Why it feels true

Here is the trap. A child who struggles with memorized procedures gets told they are not a math kid. They believe it, so they stop trying. They stop trying, so they fall further behind. The label looks more true every year, and everyone involved was kind the whole time.

You can hear the label landing. The child starts saying "I'm bad at math" as a plain fact, the way they would say their shoe size. They rush through math homework to make it stop. They guess instead of look. None of that is about ability. All of it is about a story they were told, and stories can be replaced.

I know, because I was in that loop until I was seventeen. The label was never a fact about me. It was a fact about the tool I had been given.

### The proof that it is a lie

I am one piece of proof. If a C student with ADHD can go to a perfect ACT, the label was never real. But you should not have to take my word for it.

In a three-year randomized trial in ordinary classrooms, published in Child Development in 2016, children given three hours a week on the mental abacus beat children given three hours a week of extra regular math, on calculation, on arithmetic, on place value. These were not gifted children. They were regular kids with the right tool.

In a five-year study of 144 children, published in the Journal of Neuroscience in 2019, the abacus group grew their visuospatial working memory, the kind that holds a picture steady, and brain scans showed the change.

And about a million Japanese children a year learn the soroban. They are not a million geniuses. They had a method.

When scientists put abacus-trained children in a scanner, the children solved problems in the regions used for pictures and hand movements, not the language regions the rest of us use. There is a picture of beads in there, doing the math.

I will tell you what the studies did not find, because you will read it somewhere and I would rather you heard it from me. They did not find a jump in IQ. What they found is the thing this book is about: a child who calculates faster and more accurately, with a bigger, steadier picture in their head. That is not a smaller prize. It is the one that shows up in every test your child will ever sit.

### The kid who cannot sit still

Now the part I care about most. The fidgety kid. The one who is "a handful." The one whose report card says "struggles to focus."

Those are the best students I have.

The abacus is math you do with your hands. The movement is the lesson. A child who cannot sit still for a worksheet can sit still for beads, because their hands are busy and their eyes have something to chase. I was that kid. The thing that got me in trouble in class was the thing that made the beads work.

Later, when the beads come off the table, the hands keep going. Experts' fingers twitch while they calculate in their heads. Stop the hands and the calculation slows. The fidgeting was never the problem. It was the engine.

If your child is that child, let them stand at the table. Let them bounce. Keep the session to ten minutes if fifteen is too many, and end it while they still want more. You are not managing a problem. You are pointing a motor at a job.

### The three-week fix

The fix is not that your child becomes a math kid in three weeks. The fix is that in three weeks, the label stops being believable. Here is what the first twenty-one days look like.

**Week one: reading the beads.** The goal of the whole first week is to read a number off the beads in a glance. One bead above the beam is worth five. Four below are worth one each. A bead counts when it touches the beam. By day three your child is setting single digits. By day seven they are reading three-digit numbers, and the first party trick has arrived: you set 694 on the beads, cover it after two seconds, and they tell you what it was.

Do not rush this week. A study of American first graders who were given the abacus for a year found most of them still could not read a multi-digit number off it at the end, and they learned almost nothing. Reading is the floor everything else stands on.

**Week two: moving beads.** Adding on a soroban is pushing beads toward the beam. Taking away is pushing them off. When the beads you need are sitting there free, you just move them. By the end of this week your child is adding and taking away two-digit numbers on the beads, left to right, faster than they could on paper. They have not learned a single trick yet. They have learned that numbers are things you can move.

**Week three: the first friend.** Here is the moment. Your child has four beads up on a rod and needs to add one. There is no free bead. School would say: you can't. The soroban says: bring the five down and send four away. Plus one is plus five, take away four.

The first time your child does that, out loud, with their hands, something in the room changes. They did a thing they could not do. They did it with a move, not a memory. And you heard them say the move.

That is the three-week fix. Around week three is when I started to like it. It is when a child finds out the label was never true.

### What the three weeks are not

They are not a test. Nothing is graded in the first three weeks except whether the beads were touched today. They are not a race against the trainer, which will happily hold your child on the same lesson for a week until it passes. And they are not the end of anything. They are the floor.

What you will notice, if you are watching, is smaller than a breakthrough and bigger than it sounds. Your child reaches for the soroban without being asked. They read a number off the beads at the store or in a game and say it out loud. They correct you. That last one is the sign. A child who corrects a parent's arithmetic has stopped believing the label.

### What you say during the three weeks

Praise the move, never the child. "You're so smart" tells a child they have a fixed amount of something. "You said plus five, take away four, and then you did it" tells a child they did a thing, and can do it again.

And do not say the answer. Ever. Chapter 8 is all about this, but it starts now, in week one, when they are staring at a rod trying to decide if that is a six or a seven. Wait. Let them count. Let them look. The look is the lesson.

## Chapter 4: All 34 bead moves, in the order a child's brain learns them

Everything your child will ever do on the abacus, and later in their head, comes down to thirty-four moves. Eight of them are the first real technique. Eighteen are carrying and borrowing. Eight are the two tricks at once. After those, there are no new rules, ever. Everything after is speed and the picture.

This chapter is all thirty-four, in the order the hands learn them fastest. Read it once so you know the shape of the road. Then let the trainer teach them one at a time.

### How the soroban works

Lay the soroban flat, beam running left to right. Above the beam, each rod has one bead worth five. Call it the heaven bead. Below the beam, each rod has four beads worth one each. Call them earth beads.

A bead counts when it touches the beam. Beads resting away from the beam are worth nothing.

So a rod showing one earth bead at the beam is 1. Four earth beads is 4. The heaven bead alone is 5. Heaven plus two earth beads is 7. Heaven plus four is 9. Every digit from 0 to 9 has its own shape, and the aim of the first week is for your child to know the shapes the way they know the letters.

Each rod is a place value. The rightmost rod you are using is the ones, the next is the tens, then the hundreds. Small dots on the beam mark every third rod so you can find the ones column fast.

To clear it, tilt the top edge up so every bead slides away from the beam, lay it flat, then run a finger along the top of the beam to push the heaven beads up.

### Reading and setting (Stage 1)

Reading is faster than setting, so it is trained first. Look at the ones, then the tens, then the hundreds, and say the whole number. The common slip is a zero in the middle: 305 has an empty tens rod, and an empty rod still takes up a place.

Aim for under three seconds a number. If it is slower than that, your child is counting beads one by one. The fix is to stop looking at beads and start seeing shapes. Seven is "heaven plus two." Four is "four up, heaven off." The trainer's peek mode shows a number for two seconds and hides it, which makes counting impossible and shapes the only way through.

Setting goes the other way. Hear or see a number and set it from the left: hundreds, tens, ones. Thumb pushes earth beads up. Index finger pushes them down and moves the heaven bead. Left to right is the order your child will add in later, so the habit starts now.

### Direct moves: when the beads are free (Stage 1)

To add on a soroban, push more beads to the beam. If the beads you need are sitting there free, move them. That is a direct add. Three plus one: one earth bead is free, push it up. One plus five: the heaven bead is free, bring it down. Six plus three: three earth beads are free.

To take away, push beads off the beam. If the beads you want to remove are touching the beam, pull them off. Eight take away three: pull three earth beads down. Seven take away five: push the heaven bead up.

Bigger numbers are just more rods. Thirty-two plus fifty-one: tens first, three plus five; then ones, two plus one. Sixty-eight take away thirteen: tens first, then ones. Always left to right.

Direct moves are not one of the thirty-four. They are the ground the thirty-four stand on. Your child spends the first stage doing nothing but direct moves and reading, and that is right. Then the question arrives that every later move is an answer to: what if the beads are not free?

### Why this order

The soroban has only four earth beads on a rod. Whenever you need a fifth, you have to trade: add the heaven bead and give back the difference. Those trades are the eight small-friend moves, and they come first because they happen inside a single rod.

A rod holds at most nine. Going past it means carrying one to the rod on the left and giving back the difference from ten. Those are the eighteen big-friend moves, and they come second because they need two rods.

Eight moves need both tricks at once: a carry where the amount to give back is not sitting on the rod. Those come last, because they are built from the first two groups.

Within each group, adding comes before taking away, because taking away is the same move run backwards and it is easier to learn a move backwards once you own it forwards.

This is the order the Japanese manuals use. It is the order the big abacus programs use. And it is the order a child's hands learn fastest, because each group reuses the one before it. Nothing in this book asks your child to learn a move that does not stand on a move they already own.

### How the trainer uses the order

Here is the part that makes the order work without you doing anything. Every drill problem in the trainer is built on a model of the soroban and run before your child ever sees it. If any step of it would need a move your child has not been taught, the problem is thrown away and another one is made. If the lesson is about plus three, the problem is kept only if plus three shows up in it.

So the drill for the first small-friend lesson never contains a carry. The drill for carrying never contains a combination move. Your child is never ambushed by a move they do not own, and never bored by a page that does not use the one they are learning. That is what it means to say the problems are picked for the lesson, and it is why you will never need to write a worksheet.

### Small friends: the eight five-moves (Stage 2)

Two numbers that make five are small friends. One and four. Two and three.

When you cannot add a number directly, add five and take away its friend. When you cannot take a number away directly, take away five and give back its friend.

| Adding | Taking away |
|---|---|
| +1 = +5 −4 | −1 = −5 +4 |
| +2 = +5 −3 | −2 = −5 +3 |
| +3 = +5 −2 | −3 = −5 +2 |
| +4 = +5 −1 | −4 = −5 +1 |

Here is the first one in slow motion. The rod shows 4 and you want to add 1. No earth bead is free. So bring the heaven bead down, which is plus five, and push four earth beads away, which is minus four. The rod shows 5. Plus one is plus five, take away four.

Same idea for plus four when only one, two or three earth beads are free: bring the five down, send one away. Three plus four: plus five, minus one, the rod shows 7. One plus four: plus five, minus one, the rod shows 5.

Plus two when fewer than two earth beads are free: plus five, minus three. Plus three when fewer than three are free: plus five, minus two. Four plus three: the five comes down, two go away, the rod shows 7.

Taking away runs it backwards. The rod shows 5 and you want to take away 1. There is no earth bead at the beam to remove. So push the heaven bead up, minus five, and bring four earth beads up, plus four. The rod shows 4. Minus one is minus five, add four. Six take away four: minus five, add one. Seven take away three: minus five, add two.

Inside a two-digit problem the friends show up on one rod and not the other, and that is where the decision lives. Thirteen plus twelve: tens, one plus one, free beads. Ones, three plus two, there is no second free bead, so friend: plus five, minus three. Forty-two plus eleven: tens, four plus one, friend. Ones, two plus one, free beads. On every rod of every problem, the question is the same. Free beads, or friend?

The trainer teaches these in pairs: plus one and plus four, then plus two and plus three, then the same for taking away, then all eight mixed into two-digit problems four lines long. Have your child say the formula out loud as they move the beads. "Plus three: add five, take away two." The words fade on their own once the hand knows the move.

### Big friends: the eighteen ten-moves (Stage 3)

Two numbers that make ten are big friends. One and nine. Two and eight. Three and seven. Four and six. Five and five.

To add a number that will not fit on the rod, take away its big friend and carry one to the rod on the left. To take away a number that is not there, add its big friend on this rod, then borrow one from the rod on the left.

| Adding | Taking away |
|---|---|
| +9 = −1 +10 | −9 = +1 −10 |
| +8 = −2 +10 | −8 = +2 −10 |
| +7 = −3 +10 | −7 = +3 −10 |
| +6 = −4 +10 | −6 = +4 −10 |
| +5 = −5 +10 | −5 = +5 −10 |
| +4 = −6 +10 | −4 = +6 −10 |
| +3 = −7 +10 | −3 = +7 −10 |
| +2 = −8 +10 | −2 = +8 −10 |
| +1 = −9 +10 | −1 = +9 −10 |

The rod shows 4 and you want to add 9. It will not fit. Take away one, the big friend of nine, and carry one to the tens. The ones rod shows 3, the tens rod shows 1. Four plus nine is thirteen. Seven plus eight: take away two, carry one, fifteen. Eight plus seven: take away three, carry one, fifteen.

Plus five is the odd one. It happens when the heaven bead is already down and you need another five: five plus five is take away five, carry one. Nine plus five, the same.

Small numbers can fail to fit too, when the rod is nearly full. Nine plus one: take away nine, carry one. Eight plus three: take away seven, carry one. Seven plus four: take away six, carry one.

Borrowing is the same move run backwards. The rod shows 13 and you want to take away 9. The ones rod holds 3, and 9 is not there. Add the big friend of nine, which is one, to the ones rod. Then borrow one from the tens, minus ten. The rod shows 4. Twelve take away eight: add two, borrow. Ten take away six: add four, borrow. Ten take away one: add nine, borrow.

The order of the two moves matters on a real soroban, and it matters more later in the head. Work the rod you are on first, then the rod to its left. For adding: take away the friend, then carry. For taking away: add the friend, then borrow. One rule for both.

Sometimes the carry itself will not fit. Ninety-nine plus one: the ones say take away nine, carry one to the tens. The tens hold nine too, so take away nine and carry again. A hundred. Each carry is just another plus one, with the same rules. A hundred take away one borrows twice the other way and lands on ninety-nine. Two hundred and five take away seven borrows from the hundreds, through the empty tens, and lands on one hundred ninety-eight.

This is the stage where the soroban starts beating fingers and paper, and five-line problems become the daily work. It is also the stage where school habits creep in, because carrying is the one thing school did teach, right to left. Insist on left to right every time. Slower for two weeks, faster for life.

### Combination moves: both friends at once (Stage 4)

Try six plus six on one rod. The big friend says: take away four, carry one. But the rod shows six as the heaven bead plus one earth bead. There is no four to take away. So use a small friend to take away the four: minus four is minus five, plus one. Put together, plus six is minus five, plus one, carry one.

| Adding | Taking away |
|---|---|
| +6 = −5 +1 +10 | −6 = +5 −1 −10 |
| +7 = −5 +2 +10 | −7 = +5 −2 −10 |
| +8 = −5 +3 +10 | −8 = +5 −3 −10 |
| +9 = −5 +4 +10 | −9 = +5 −4 −10 |

Seven plus seven: minus five, plus two, carry. Fourteen. Five plus nine: minus five, plus four, carry. Fourteen. Eight plus six: minus five, plus one, carry. Fourteen.

Eleven take away six runs it backwards. Add four, the big friend of six, to the ones, then borrow one from the tens. The ones rod shows 1, and one plus four needs a small friend: plus five, minus one. So minus six is plus five, minus one, borrow one. Twelve take away seven: plus five, minus two, borrow. Fourteen take away nine: plus five, minus four, borrow.

These eight only happen in a few situations: adding six to nine onto a rod showing five to eight, or taking six to nine away from a rod showing one to four with a borrow. The trainer finds those situations for your child so they get the reps.

Watch the order on a combination add: heaven bead up, earth beads up, then carry. Three moves, one formula, said in one breath. "Plus seven: take away five, add two, carry one."

### What the moves sound like

Every move has a spoken line, and the trainer shows it under the beads. Small friends: "Plus three: add five, take away two." "Minus four: take away five, add one." Big friends: "Plus seven: take away three, carry one to the left." "Minus eight: add two, borrow one from the left." Both at once: "Plus seven: take away five, add two, carry one."

The lines matter more than they look. A child who says the line is a child whose hand is about to do the right thing, because the words run a half-step ahead of the fingers and tell them where to go. In the first week of any group the line is said out loud. In the second it is whispered. By the third it has gone quiet, and the hand simply moves, which is what automatic means.

### How a stacked problem runs

A problem four lines long is run the way an abacus user runs it, not the way school does. Set the first number on the beads. Add or take away each line that follows, left to right across the rods, one line at a time. The paper only ever holds the problem; the beads hold the answer, and your child reads it off at the end. Nothing is written down in between. The eyes stay a line ahead of the hand, and the answer is not checked until the last line is done.

### After the thirty-fourth move

Eight small friends. Eighteen big friends. Eight combinations. Thirty-four.

When the last one is automatic, nothing new ever happens on a rod again. Three digits instead of two? Three rods, same moves. Four hundred sixty-eight plus three hundred fifty-seven is hundreds, then tens, then ones, with the carries running across all three. Ten rows of numbers instead of four? Same moves, more of them, with the eyes kept one line ahead of the hand and the answer never checked until the end. Multiplication, later, is times tables plus these same adds. Division is guess, multiply, and these same take-aways.

On every rod of every problem your child will ever do, the question is the same four-step question, and it never gets a fifth step. Are the beads free? If not, is there a small friend? If it will not fit, big friend. If the friend is not there, both at once.

From here on, fluency is the whole game. And fluency is the thing that lets the beads come off the table, because a hand that no longer has to think about the move leaves the mind free to hold the picture.

## Chapter 5: The ten stages, Sprout to Legend

The thirty-four moves are the first four stages. There are ten. Here is the whole road, so you know where your child is standing at any point and what the next landmark looks like.

| Stage | By the end, your child can | About |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Sprout | Read any number off the beads in a glance, and add or take away whenever the beads are free | 2–3 weeks |
| 2 Explorer | Use the make-five trick to add and subtract two-digit numbers on the beads | 3–4 weeks |
| 3 Apprentice | Carry and borrow between rods with the make-ten trick; five-line sums on the beads | 5–6 weeks |
| 4 Adept | Every bead move automatic; three-digit sums ten rows long; the first real sums in the head | 5–6 weeks |
| 5 Ace | Fast hands on the beads; five two-digit numbers added in the head; the first flash round and the first Bead Score | 6–8 weeks |
| 6 Wizard | Multiply on the beads, and two-digit times one-digit in the head | 6–8 weeks |
| 7 Champion | Divide on the beads and in the head; three-digit sums in the head begin | 6–8 weeks |
| 8 Prodigy | Ten three-digit numbers added in the head; three-digit flash; two-digit times two-digit mentally | 3–4 months |
| 9 Grandmaster | Ten three-digit numbers flashed at half a second each; four-digit sums in the head | 4–6 months |
| 10 Legend | Fifteen numbers flashed at a third of a second; three-digit times three-digit in the head | open-ended |

### How a stage feels

Sprout is when the beads stop being a toy and start being numbers. Explorer is the first real technique, and the week the hands start moving without being told. Apprentice is when the soroban becomes faster than fingers and paper. Adept is the end of new rules: everything after is speed and the picture.

Ace is when other people start to notice, because the beads are gone and the answers are still coming. Wizard turns times tables from a chore into a tool. Champion is all four operations, which is more than most adults can do in their heads. Prodigy is expert-level mental arithmetic and the stage to film. Grandmaster targets sit around the Japanese dan grades, the ones club kids reach after years. Legend is competition speed, and there is no top to that ladder.

### The later stages, in a paragraph each

Multiplication on the soroban is times tables plus adding. Thirty-four times seven is three times seven and four times seven, each small product added in the right place, left to right, and the answer builds itself. The only new skill is knowing where each product lands, and the first lesson of the stage is a times-table test, because the facts have to be instant before the beads can use them.

Division is multiplication run backwards: guess a digit, multiply, take away, repeat. Nine hundred fifty-two divided by seven is one seven in nine, three sevens in twenty-five, six sevens in forty-two. Everything your child needs for it is already in their hands.

Decimals and money change nothing on the beads. Pick a rod to be the ones and the rods to its right are tenths and hundredths, and a shopping receipt becomes a drill. A total that drops below zero is handled by borrowing from an imaginary rod on the far left, which is rare in exams and useful in life.

By the eighth stage the wooden soroban is a warm-up tool and every drill is mental. Ten mixed three-digit lines in the head is the format of the top Japanese mental calculation exam below the dan grades, and that is the stage to film.

### How the trainer moves your child along

Every lesson is the same shape. A short explanation and a bead demo you press play on, two minutes. Then a drill of ten problems, each one chosen so it needs only the moves your child already knows, plus the one being learned. Nine out of ten right and the lesson is done. Not yet? Same drill tomorrow.

Every stage ends with a boss: a short timed test in two or three parts. Pass it and your child earns the badge and the next stage. Bosses are meant to take two or three tries. Each part maps to one or two lessons, so when a part fails you know exactly which drill to repeat.

Every right answer earns points, and mental answers earn more. There are levels, and a streak of days in a row, and a daily quest of three short drills with a bonus for finishing all three. Kids care about the level more than you would expect, and on a busy day the quest alone is enough to keep the streak alive.

### How long it takes

At fifteen to twenty minutes a day, six days a week, a child of seven or older reaches Prodigy in roughly fourteen to eighteen months. The Grandmaster targets take another half year or more. Younger children, or a few sessions a week, take longer, and that is fine. The chart still climbs.

The big classroom programs cover the same ground in two to four years, because they are weekly and in groups. This is daily and one-on-one, and the difference is not the content. It is the number of minutes your child's hands are on the beads.

Stage 1 is unlocked with your kit. The rest of the road lives in Child Phenom University, and the last chapter tells you how to get there. But for now, Sprout is enough. Sprout is the stage that decides everything.

## Chapter 6: Flash rounds at the dinner table

Picture this. Sunday dinner, grandparents at the table. You say five numbers out loud, one a second, and your eight-year-old says the total before you have put your fork down. Then you do it again with two-digit numbers. Then your father-in-law tries to catch them out and cannot.

That is a flash round. It is the single most convincing thing your child will ever do with this method, and it costs nothing to run. This chapter is how the picture gets built, and how to turn it into the game.

### The picture

Go back to the first week. Your child looks at a number on the beads for one second. They close their eyes. Can they still see it? They say it.

That picture is called the mental abacus. Experts do all their math on it. Your child's is tiny at first, one rod, a few beads. Every stage makes it bigger. By the fifth stage it holds two rods and five lines of numbers. By the eighth it holds three rods and ten lines, which is the format of the top Japanese mental calculation exam below the dan grades.

The picture is real, and we know it in three ways. When trained abacus users calculate, brain scans light up in the regions for pictures and hand movements, not the regions for words. When they make a mistake, it is often off by exactly five, which is one heaven bead in the wrong place. And they can calculate while talking about something else, because the words are not where the math is happening.

The picture also has a size. Research on mental-abacus users finds it holds about three or four rods at once, the same limit as the number of objects anyone can track with their eyes. That is why two-digit mental sums feel like a jump: your child is now holding two objects, each with its own beads.

### Keeping the picture alive

The picture is held by the hands as much as the eyes. When your child first goes mental, their fingers will twitch on the table as if the beads were there. Let them. Studies of mental-abacus learners show that stopping the hands slows the calculation. The fingers are not cheating. They are the engine, and most children stop using them on their own as the picture gets stronger.

The enemy of the picture is words. If your child catches themselves saying "sixty-two" in their head, the picture has collapsed into language, and language is too slow. The fix is always the same: do the same sum once on the real soroban, watch it, close your eyes, replay it, then try the next one in the head.

### What flash anzan is

A Japanese teacher named Yoji Miyamoto invented flash anzan to force his students off the physical abacus. Numbers flash on a screen one after another and vanish. The student keeps the running total on the mental beads and says only the final answer. You cannot write anything down, you cannot go back, and the numbers come too fast to say, so the picture has to do all the work.

Champions add fifteen three-digit numbers flashed in under two seconds total. The world record is 1.61 seconds, set in 2024. Asked afterward what the numbers were, the record holders cannot tell you. Only the total ever existed in their mind.

That is also why flash beats a worksheet. On paper a child can write the running total, glance back, count on fingers. In a flash round there is nothing to glance back at. The only place the total can live is on the mental beads, so that is where it goes, and the picture grows because it has to.

Your child starts at five one-digit numbers, each showing for a second and a half. In the trainer, that is the fifth stage. At the dinner table, it starts sooner, because you are the flash machine.

### The dinner-table version

You say the numbers. Your child keeps the total on their mental beads and says one thing at the end: the answer.

Keep a steady beat. One number a second is a good start, and it is slower than it sounds. Say each number once, cleanly, and do not repeat it. The whole point is that a number lands and is gone.

Your child says nothing until the end. Not the running total, not the numbers back to you. If a round falls apart, let it go. Start fresh on the next one. A lost round costs nothing, and a child who learns to let one go is a child who can take the next number anyway and still be right more often than you would think.

Breathe out as each number lands. That is the feeling to aim for: calm. The number arrives, the beads move, it is gone. Nothing to hold onto but the picture.

Here is what the first one sounds like, tonight, with a child in the second stage. You say "Ready?" and wait for the nod. "Three." A beat. "Five." A beat. "One." Your child says "nine." You say "yes," and nothing else, and do the next one. Three rounds is a session. Three rounds a night, every night, is the whole game.

### What numbers to say

The head can only use a move the hand already owns. So the numbers you say in a flash round must need only the moves your child has learned on the beads.

In the first two stages that means free-bead moves only. On one rod, that is numbers like three, then five, then one: nothing that needs a trade. Three small numbers that add to nine or less, with no step that bumps into the heaven bead or runs past nine. The workbook prints a round a day already checked, and the trainer's flash drills do it on screen. If you are making up your own, run each number on the beads in your own head first, and if it would need a trick your child has not learned, pick a different number.

Once the small friends are automatic, the rounds can use them. Once carrying is automatic, the totals can cross ten. By the fifth stage, anything goes.

Here is the ladder, roughly in the order the trainer walks it, with a round you can say at each level.

| Level | The round | Say this | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Three one-digit numbers, free beads only, adding | 3, 5, 1 → 9 | Stage 2 |
| 2 | Three one-digit numbers, free beads only, plus and minus | 7, minus 1, 2 → 8 | Stage 2 |
| 3 | Three one-digit numbers that need small friends | 4, 3, 1 → 8 | Stage 3 |
| 4 | Four one-digit numbers, the total crossing ten | 8, 7, minus 3, 4 → 16 | Stage 3 |
| 5 | Five one-digit numbers, every move allowed | 6, 8, minus 9, 7, minus 5 → 7 | Stage 4 |
| 6 | Two, then three two-digit numbers | 34, 28, 17 → 79 | Stage 4 |
| 7 | Five two-digit numbers, plus and minus | 47, minus 18, 36, 25, minus 41 → 49 | Stage 5 |
| 8 | Three three-digit numbers | 386, 457, 229 → 1,072 | Stage 7 |
| 9 | Ten three-digit numbers, mixed | the trainer makes these | Stage 8 |

Every example on that table was run on the beads before it was printed, and uses only the moves of its stage. The trainer flashes numbers on screen with the timing exact. The table version is the same game with your voice, and it goes anywhere: the car, the bath, the walk to school.

### Two-digit rounds

When the rounds move to two-digit numbers, each number lands on two rods at once: tens, then ones, in the order it is said. "Thirty-four" is a three on the tens rod and a four on the ones, and the picture takes it as one shape, not two digits. A minus is part of the number. Say "minus eighteen" as one chunk and let your child take it as one move, the way the trainer shows it on screen. A child who hears "minus" and then waits for the number has already lost the beat.

### Speeding up

Start at one number a second and stay there until your child is right nine times out of ten. Then go a little faster. Not because speed is the goal, but because speed is what forces the picture: at one number every seven-tenths of a second the words cannot keep up, and the beads have to take over.

The mistake nearly every parent makes is speeding up too soon. A child who is right half the time at a fast beat is not building the picture, they are guessing. A child who is right nine times out of ten at a slow beat is building it, and the beat will follow. Accuracy first, always, and the clock takes care of itself.

### Why the dinner table

You could run flash rounds alone in the kitchen, and you will, most nights. But the table matters, and here is why.

In chapter 3 the label was the problem: a story about your child, told by other people, in front of other people. The dinner table is where the story gets rewritten, in front of the same kind of audience. A child who takes five numbers while the adults watch, and gets it, has just become the person at the table who can do a thing the adults cannot. Nobody has to say it. Everybody saw it.

That is also why a lost round in front of people is worth more than a won round alone. The child shrugs, asks for another, and takes it. The adults have watched the picture hold up under pressure, and so has the child, which is the thing they will remember on the morning of a test.

### Dictation: the oldest version of the game

Abacus schools in Japan run their mental drills by voice. The teacher calls numbers, the class answers. It is the same thing you are doing at the table, and it works on the beads as well as in the head. Read a list of numbers at a steady pace while your child moves the beads. Then read the same list with the soroban pushed away.

Dictation is also the version for a child who is not yet mental. In the first stage, read two-digit numbers aloud and let your child set them. In the second, read a four-line problem and let them work it on the beads with nothing written down. The eyes are off the page and on the beads, which is where they will need to be later.

The trainer has a listening mode that does this with a computer voice, and it means your child can practice with any parent, grandparent or babysitter who can read a list. Nobody needs to know any math. They need to be able to count to a beat.

### The grandparent show

The first show is reading. Set a three-digit number on the beads, cover it after two seconds, and let your child say it. That is available in week one, and it is already more than most adults can do.

The second show is eyes closed. Show a number, cover it, and let your child say it with their eyes shut. Week two.

The third show is the flash round, and it arrives properly in the fifth stage, when the trainer starts flashing numbers on screen. By then your child can take five two-digit numbers by voice. Grandparents do not recover from that one.

Someone at the table will try to catch your child out. They will say the numbers faster, or throw in a big one. Let them. Tell them the only rule is one number at a time, said once. Your child will take it, or lose the round and shrug and ask for another. Either way the adult has learned something about the picture, and your child has learned that the picture holds up under pressure, which is the whole point of doing it in front of people.

Film it. A thirty-second clip of a child taking five numbers and saying the total is the thing that gets sent to every relative you have. And it goes on the fridge next to the Bead Score, which is the next chapter.

### What your child will tell you

Three things, usually in this order, and each one means the picture has grown.

"I can see it." The first time a child says this they are surprised, and they mean it literally: a rod with beads on it, behind the eyes, that moves when the numbers come. This is the mental abacus arriving, and it usually comes in the second or third stage during the eyes-closed drills.

"My fingers did it." The child noticed their own hand twitching on the table during a mental sum, and noticed that the sum went better when it did. That is the motor half of the picture switching on, and it is the reason the experts' fingers move too.

"I don't know what the numbers were. I just know the answer." This is the flash round working exactly as it should. The numbers landed, moved the beads, and vanished. Only the total was ever stored. When your child says this, they are describing the same thing the world record holders describe, and the only difference between them now is the beat.

### How far the ladder goes

For the curious, here is the Japanese flash anzan ladder, which the trainer's top stages are calibrated to.

| 20th kyu | Three one-digit numbers | 5 seconds |

## Chapter 7: The Bead Score

Every parent I talk to asks the same question in a different way. How is my child doing, really?

Not "did they pass the lesson." Not "did they like it today." Where do they stand. The Bead Score is the answer: one number, taken with no soroban and no paper, that measures only the mental skill, and shows where it sits for a child of that age.

### What it is

The Bead Score is a ladder with twenty rungs. The bottom rung is adding two small numbers in the head. The top rung is fifteen three-digit numbers flashed at a fifth of a second each. The test starts at the bottom, climbs one rung at a time, and stops when a rung fails. The score is the top rung cleared.

| Rung | Your child can | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Add two small numbers, no crossing ten | The picture exists |
| 2 | Add any two digits, like 7 + 8 | Carries in the head |
| 3 | Add three one-digit numbers | Keeps a running total |
| 4 | Add a two-digit and a one-digit number, like 47 + 8 | Two rods in the picture |
| 5 | Add two two-digit numbers with carrying, like 38 + 27 | Carries between rods, in the head |
| 6 | Three two-digit numbers, plus and minus | Most adults top out here |
| 7 | Flash: five one-digit numbers at one second | Mental speed begins; about 18th kyu on the Japanese flash ladder |
| 8 | Five two-digit numbers added | Beyond what any school grade asks for |
| 9 | Five two-digit numbers, plus and minus | Most adults reach for a calculator here |
| 10 | Three three-digit numbers added | Hundreds in the head |
| 11 | Flash: five two-digit numbers at one second | About 7th kyu; the picture is doing the work |
| 12 | Five three-digit numbers, plus and minus | The Japanese 3rd-kyu mental exam format |
| 13 | Flash: eight two-digit numbers at 0.7 seconds | Faster than most people can read the numbers |
| 14 | Flash: five three-digit numbers at one second | Three rods held at once; around 2nd dan |
| 15 | Ten three-digit numbers, plus and minus | The Japanese 1st-kyu mental exam, top grade below dan |
| 16 | Flash: ten three-digit numbers at 0.7 seconds | About 5th dan; competition entry |
| 17 | Flash: ten three-digit numbers at 0.5 seconds | Around 7th dan |
| 18 | Flash: fifteen three-digit numbers at 0.4 seconds | National-competition territory |
| 19 | Flash: fifteen three-digit numbers at 0.3 seconds | Very few people on Earth |
| 20 | Flash: fifteen three-digit numbers at 0.2 seconds | The world record is about half this |

Look at rung six. Three two-digit numbers, plus and minus, in the head. That is where most adults top out, including most of the adults who will be at your dinner table. Your child passes it somewhere in the fourth or fifth stage.

### The Times ladder

There is a second ladder, which opens in the sixth stage when multiplication starts. Ten rungs, from times tables to three-digit times three-digit in the head.

| Rung | Your child can |
|---|---|
| 1 | Times tables to five |
| 2 | Times tables to nine, fast |
| 3 | Two-digit times one-digit, like 47 × 6, in the head |
| 4 | Two-digit divided by one-digit, like 84 ÷ 7 |
| 5 | Three-digit times one-digit, like 386 × 7 |
| 6 | Three-digit divided by one-digit, like 532 ÷ 7 |
| 7 | Two-digit times two-digit, like 47 × 36; most adults need paper |
| 8 | Four-digit divided by two-digit, like 2,752 ÷ 32 |
| 9 | Three-digit times two-digit, like 386 × 47 |
| 10 | Three-digit times three-digit, like 386 × 472; dan level |

### How to take it

The test lives in the trainer. Your child takes the first one at the end of the fifth stage, when the mental drills and the first flash rounds have given the picture something to stand on. Before that, the lessons themselves are the measure.

No soroban. No paper. No help from you. The point of the number is that it measures the picture and nothing else. Sit beside your child as usual, and say nothing until the test is over.

A test takes about ten minutes. The early rungs feel easy, then one feels like work, then one does not go, and that is the score. The stop is not a failure. It is the ladder doing its job, which is to find the edge of the picture and mark it.

Then write the number down. The first Bead Score is the one your child will beat, and it is worth having it on the fridge in your child's own handwriting.

### How often

Every two to four weeks, in place of the day's mental drill. Not more often. The ladder does not move in a week, and a child who tests every few days learns to dread the test instead of chase it.

Never test tired, and never test on a bad day. The number should be the best honest picture, and a child who is cranky at bedtime will test two rungs under where they live. If a score comes in lower than the last one, that is what happened, and the fix is a normal fortnight and a retake.

Between tests, the daily mental drill stays the same size. Plateaus are normal. They last about two weeks and they break overnight, usually after a day when nothing seemed to happen. Keep the drill the same, keep the sessions short, and let the test every few weeks show what the days did not.

### The chart

Each Bead Score goes on a chart in the trainer, and the chart shows two things. Your child's own line, climbing test by test. And shaded bands for where children of the same age typically sit, built from what each school grade expects of mental arithmetic. The line is the honest number; the bands tell you how far ahead of the room your child has moved.

For most seven-year-olds, the typical rung is around two. For nine-year-olds, around four. For adults, around six. By the end of the fifth stage, your child is past the adult line, and the chart makes that visible in a way a report card never will.

Read it the way you would read a growth chart. A nine-year-old on rung five is ahead of most of the class. On rung eight, they are past the adults. The number that matters most is not where the line is, but that it keeps going up, test after test, and it will, as long as the days keep happening.

### What to do with the number

Three things.

Put it where the child can see it. The fridge, the bedroom door, the inside of the soroban's lid. A number a child can see is a number a child will chase.

Say it to the grandparents. Not "she's doing well in math." "She's on rung eight. That's five two-digit numbers added in her head. Try it." They will try it. They will not get there. Your child will watch them not get there.

And film the test every few months. The clip of a child taking a flash rung is the proof. The number is the score.

## Chapter 8: What to say when they get stuck (never the answer)

This is the chapter you will come back to.

Your child is sitting at the beads. A rod shows four. The next number is plus one. There is no free bead, and they are looking at it, and the seconds are stretching, and every instinct you have is to say "bring the five down."

Do not.

### Why never the answer

The decision is the skill. Not the bead move, the decision. Free beads, or friend? Every time your child makes that decision themselves, a little more of it moves from thinking into knowing. Every time you make it for them, you take that repetition away and give them a worse one: wait for Mom.

A child who hears the answer learns that stuck means someone else will fix it. A child who is asked a question learns that stuck means look again. The second child is the one who, five years from now, sits down to a test they have never seen and starts.

So there is one question, and you ask it every time. Free beads, or friend? Then you wait, for longer than is comfortable. The silence is where the learning is.

Keep your hands in your lap. It sounds small. It is the single hardest part of the job, because your hand will want to reach for the bead the moment you see the move. A parent whose hands stay in their lap is a parent whose child owns the beads.

### The lines, stage by stage

Sprout. Say every number out loud with your child. Stop while it is still fun.

Explorer. When they hesitate, ask "free beads, or friend?" and wait. Do not touch the beads.

Apprentice. Carries feel backwards for a week. Insist on left to right every time.

Adept. Praise the formula said out loud more than the right answer.

Ace. Write the day's time on the fridge. Kids love watching a number fall.

Wizard. Times tables five minutes a day for two weeks before the first multiply lesson pays off.

Champion. Division is slow at first. Accuracy, then speed.

Prodigy. Plateaus last two weeks and break overnight. Keep the daily drill at the same size.

Grandmaster. Short and daily beats long and weekly, more than ever.

Legend. Film it. Then show the grandparents.

### When they are counting beads one by one

Reading is slow because the child is counting, not seeing. Go back to the peek drill in the first stage and shorten the peek until counting is impossible. Each digit has a shape. "What does seven look like?" is the question, and the answer is not a number, it is a picture: heaven plus two.

### When the school method creeps in

Right-to-left habits come back, especially after a carry lesson, because carrying is the one thing school taught and school taught it backwards. You will see your child's eyes jump to the ones column first. Say "left to right" and nothing else. Every time. Slower for two weeks, faster for life.

### When they forget a formula mid-problem

Normal in the first week of any new group of moves. The trainer has a hint button for exactly this, and there is no shame in it. The line to say is "what's the friend of three?" and let them find it.

If it lasts past a week, the problem is not this formula. It is the group before it, which is not automatic yet. Go back one lesson and repeat its drill until it passes three days running. Then come forward.

### When they rush

A child who is getting seven out of ten at speed has decided that fast is the point. It is not, and the line is "slow is fine." Say it flat, as a fact, not a correction. Then time nothing for a week. When the accuracy is back at nine out of ten, bring the clock back, and watch the time fall on its own.

### When they are stuck at a boss

Bosses are meant to take two or three tries. Look at which part failed. Each part maps to one or two lessons in the stage, so you know exactly which drill to repeat. Three days on those lessons, then try the boss again. Say "the boss is just the lessons, in a row, with a clock." That is all it is.

### When the mental drills stop improving

Two-week plateaus are standard. They break overnight. Keep the daily mental drill at the same size, do not make it bigger to force progress, and add dictation for variety: you read the numbers, they answer. The line to say is "same as yesterday." A plateau is the brain finishing a floor before it builds the next one.

### When the picture collapses

Your child says "I lost it" halfway through a mental sum. The picture turned into words, and words are too slow. Do the same sum once on the real soroban. Watch it. Close your eyes. Replay it. Then the next one in the head. "Back to the beads, then back to your eyes" is the whole instruction.

### When they want to quit

Every child says it once, usually in the third stage, when carrying is new and the beads feel slower than fingers. Shorten the session. Drop to the daily quest alone, which keeps the streak alive in five minutes. Do not add anything, do not bargain, and do not make a speech about perseverance. The feeling passes in a week, when the carries become automatic and the soroban is suddenly faster than anything, and the child who wanted to quit is the child who will not stop.

### When there are tears

Stop for the day, warmly. Shorten tomorrow's session. The streak only needs one drill to stay alive, so nothing is lost. A child who links the beads with pressure will not build a picture of them, and the picture is the only thing that matters. "We're done for today. Good work. Same time tomorrow." Then put the soroban away without a sigh.

### When you are the one who is stuck

You will have a day when your patience is gone before the warm-up is. Hand the chair to someone else. A dictation round needs only an adult who can read a list, so the other parent, a grandparent on the phone, an older sibling can take the day. Or do the quest alone and let the trainer be the coach. The chair is the method, but the chair does not have to be you every single day.

And if your child asks a question you cannot answer, say so and press the trainer's "show me" button together. The beads will do the move on screen. You were never supposed to know the answer. You were supposed to sit there.

### When your child is under six

Expect the first two stages to take twice as long. Ten-minute sessions. Use the screen abacus more than the wooden one. If reading numbers has not clicked after a month, pause for a few months and come back. The window is wide.

### The praise that works

"You said the formula before you moved." "You went left to right without me saying it." "You finished the whole problem before you checked." "You let that round go and took the next one."

Every one of those names a thing the child did and can do again. None of them names a thing the child is. That is the whole difference, and it is the difference between a child who tries the next hard thing and a child who protects a label.

### The three stuck lines

If you remember nothing else from this chapter, remember these, because between them they cover almost every moment at the beads.

"A bead only counts when it touches the beam." For any reading or setting trouble.

"Free beads, or friend?" For any add or take-away trouble, at every stage, forever.

"Let it go. Next one." For any flash round or mental sum that falls apart.

And the thing you never say, which is the answer. Your child will get the answer. They will get it slower than you could give it, and it will be theirs, and that is the point of the whole thing.

## The science, in plain words

**It builds arithmetic speed and accuracy.** Three randomized trials, with ordinary children in India and China and with older students, found abacus-trained learners faster and more accurate at arithmetic than learners given extra regular math. The Indian trial ran three years in school classrooms, three hours a week, and found large gains in calculation and place value. The Chinese study followed 144 children for five years and found gains in arithmetic and in the visuospatial working memory that holds the picture steady. A third, shorter study of fifty-two hours of training found gains in calculation and spatial skills.

**It uses a different part of the brain.** Brain scans of trained abacus users show calculation lighting up the regions for pictures and hand movements, not the language regions the rest of us use. They can calculate while talking, because the words are not where the math is.

**The picture is a real abacus.** When experts slip, they are often off by exactly five, one heaven bead in the wrong place. The picture holds about three or four rods at once. Stopping the hands slows it down.

**What the studies did not find.** No jump in IQ, and no general gain in memory or attention beyond the picture itself. The gains are in arithmetic and in the working memory that holds the beads. And in a one-year American trial with first graders who had not yet learned place value, most could not read the beads at the end and gained nothing. That is why Stage 1 is slow, and why your child should be able to count to a hundred before starting.

**The ladders are calibrated to real exams.** The stage targets and the Bead Score rungs are set against the Japanese bead and mental calculation exam grades and the flash anzan ladder, so a rung in the trainer means what it would mean in a club in Japan.
