## 6 · Off by five

- **Subject:** The mistake that proves it works

Hi {{first_name}},

Here is a strange fact about kids who are good at the abacus.

When they make a mistake in their head, it's often off by exactly five.

They say 47 when the answer is 42. They say 380 when it's 330.

Why five? Because of the top bead. On a soroban, the bead above the beam is worth five. If the picture in the head drops that bead in the wrong place, the answer is off by five.

James Stigler saw this back in 1984. It's one of the first clues that these kids really do see an abacus in their heads. A kid counting on fingers would not make that kind of slip.

So the next time your child is off by five, don't sigh. Smile a little. It means the picture is in there. It just needs to sharpen up.

Then ask: "Check your top bead."

## 10 · The one question

## 11 · Never the answer

- **Subject:** Don't say the answer

Hi {{first_name}},

This one is hard for parents.

When your child is stuck, don't say the answer.

I know. They're sitting there. The clock is going. You can see it. Eight. It's eight. Just say eight.

Don't.

If you say it, they get one problem right and learn one thing: wait long enough and a grown-up will save you.

If you wait, they learn something better. "I can get unstuck."

So what do you do instead? Three small things, one at a time:

- Ask "Free beads, or friend?"
- If that's not enough, point at the rod. Say nothing.
- If they're still stuck, clear the beads and start that problem over.

A finished problem, checked and redone, beats a saved one. Every time.

## 13 · The zero in the middle

- **Subject:** Why 305 trips kids up

Hi {{first_name}},

Set 305 on the beads. Ask your child to read it.

Lots of kids say "35."

The tens rod is empty, so their eyes skip it. But an empty rod still takes up a place. That zero matters.

This is the most common slip in the first week. It looks tiny. It isn't.

A kid who skips the zero on the beads will skip it on paper too. Then 305 becomes 35, and a whole word problem goes wrong, and they decide they're "bad at math."

The fix is simple. Point to the empty rod and wait. Let them find it.

Then play a game. You set a number with a zero in it. They read it. They set one. You read it. Make a mistake on purpose. See if they catch you.

They will. And they'll love it.

## 14 · Left to right

- **Subject:** Why we add the wrong way round

Hi {{first_name}},

School teaches kids to add from the right. Ones first, then carry to the tens.

On the soroban, we go the other way. Left to right. Hundreds, then tens, then ones.

The first time a parent sees this, they think it's wrong. It's not. It's the reason mental math works.

Think about how you say a number. Three hundred forty-two. You say the big part first. When your child adds left to right, they add in the same order they say the number. So they can keep up in their head.

Right to left on paper is fine for paper. But you can't hold a carry in your head while reading a number backward. Left to right, you can.

So if your child's school sheet and their abacus sheet look different, that's on purpose. They're learning two tools. One for paper. One for the head.

## 17 · Three or four rods

Hi {{first_name}},

Here's something parents don't expect.

In a 2012 study, Frank and Barner found that kids doing mental abacus math could only hold about three or four rods in their heads. Not thirteen. Three or four.

That's why the mental part of each lesson starts with tiny sums. One rod. Then two. Then three.

If your child can do big sums on the beads but freezes when the beads go away, nothing is wrong. Their picture is still small. It grows a rod at a time.

The same goes for you, by the way. Try to see seven rods with your eyes closed. Hard, isn't it?

So be patient with the mental drills. Keep them short. Five minutes at the end of each session. Never skip them. That's where the picture grows.

## 18 · Flash at dinner

- **Subject:** A game for the dinner table

Hi {{first_name}},

Here is a game for tonight. It takes two minutes. It works best when grandparents are around.

Write five small numbers on cards. One digit each. Hold up one card at a time, about a second each. Then ask your child for the total.

That's a flash round. It's the same drill the flash anzan exams use, only slower.

At first, your child may need the beads beside them. Fine. Then try it with the beads pushed away. Then try six cards. Then faster.

Let your child flash the cards for you, too. Let them watch you struggle. Kids love beating a grown-up.

This is the part of the method that looks like magic from the outside. A kid hears or sees a list of numbers and just knows the sum. From the inside, it's a picture of beads, moving fast.
