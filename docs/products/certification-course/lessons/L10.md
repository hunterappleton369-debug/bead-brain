# L10 · Teaching the order, and the one question

Why the 34 moves are taught in this order, how to tell when a child is ready for the next group, and the question that does the teaching for you.

**Week** 3 · **Video** <!-- runtime:start -->about 6 min<!-- runtime:end --> · **Lesson time** 75 min · **Mirrors** Stages 2 to 4 as a whole, and Playbook chapter 4 ("Why this order") and chapter 8 ("Why never the answer")

## Video script

[HUNTER: talking head, soroban on the table. Show the formula card on screen when you list the groups.]

You now know all thirty-four moves. Today is about teaching them. And the first thing to teach is the order.

### Why this order

The soroban has four earth beads on a rod. Whenever you need a fifth, you trade. Add the heaven bead and give back the difference. Those trades are the eight small friends. They come first, because they happen inside one rod.

A rod holds nine at most. Going past it means carrying to the next rod. Those are the eighteen big friends. They come second, because they need two rods.

Eight moves need both at once. They come last, because they're built from the first two groups.

[on screen: Small friends (1 rod) → Big friends (2 rods) → Both at once (built from both)]

Inside each group, adding comes before taking away. Taking away is the same move run backwards. It's easier to learn a move backwards once you own it forwards.

This is the order the Japanese manuals use, and the order the big abacus programs use. Each group stands on the one before it. Nothing asks a child to learn a move that doesn't stand on a move they already own.

### When is a child ready for the next group?

The rule of thumb is three days. When the lesson's drill passes three days running, the child owns the move. Move on.

If a child keeps forgetting a move after a week, look one group back. The problem is almost never the new move. It's the old group, which isn't automatic yet. Go back. Repeat its drill until it passes three days in a row. Then come forward.

### Problems picked for the lesson

The trainer builds every problem on a model of the soroban. It runs the problem before the child ever sees it. If any step needs a move the child hasn't learned, the problem is thrown out. If the lesson is about plus three, the problem is kept only if plus three shows up in it.

So a child is never ambushed by a move they don't own. And they're never bored by a page that doesn't use the one they're learning. That's why you never need to write a worksheet. Use the trainer and the printed sheets. They're built for this.

### The one question

Now the most important thing you'll do as a teacher. You won't explain. You'll ask.

[on screen: "Free beads, or friend?"]

The decision is the skill. Not the bead move. The decision. Every time a child makes it, a little more of it moves from thinking into knowing. Every time you make it for them, you take that rep away. You give them a worse one: wait for the teacher.

A child who hears the answer learns that stuck means someone else will fix it. A child who is asked a question learns that stuck means look again.

So there's one question, and you ask it every time. "Free beads, or friend?" Then you wait. Longer than is comfortable. The silence is where the learning is.

### Your hands

Keep your hands in your lap. It sounds small. It's the hardest part of the job. Your hand will want to reach for the bead the second you see the move. A teacher whose hands stay in their lap has a student who owns the beads.

### Out loud, whisper, silent

Every move has a spoken line. "Plus three: add five, take away two." The words run a half step ahead of the fingers.

In the first week of a new group, the line is said out loud. In the second week, whispered. By the third, it's gone quiet, and the hand just moves.

In a class, have the kids say the line together for the first week. A room saying "plus three: add five, take away two" is a room where every hand is about to do the right thing.

### The colors

The trainer colors every move. Free beads are green. Small friends are orange. Big friends are blue. Both at once is purple.

Use the same colors on anything you make. A child learns "orange means friend" faster than any word you can say.

### What you praise

Name what the child did, not what they are. "You said the formula before you moved." "You went left to right without me saying it." "You finished the whole problem before you checked."

Each of those is a thing they can do again. "You're so smart" is a label, and kids protect labels. They stop trying hard things.

### Check answers

1. Small friends happen inside one rod. Big friends need two rods.
2. A take-away is the same move run backwards, and it is easier to learn backwards once you own it forwards.
3. Go back one group (the big-friend adds, or the small friends if those are shaky). Repeat that drill until it passes three days running, then come forward.
4. For example: "You said the formula before you moved." "You went left to right without me saying it." "You're so smart" names a label, not a thing the child did, and kids protect labels by avoiding hard things.
5. Free beads green, small friend orange, big friend blue, both at once purple.
