# L01 · Why this works, and what you may promise

The lesson that lets you explain the method to any parent in two minutes, and never say a thing you cannot back up.

**Week** 1 · **Video** <!-- runtime:start -->about 7 min<!-- runtime:end --> · **Lesson time** 45 min · **Mirrors** Playbook chapters 1 and 3, and "The science, in plain words"

## Video script

[HUNTER: record as a talking head at the kitchen table, soroban on the table. On-screen text cues are in brackets.]

Welcome to the course. I'm Hunter.

Here is what you get by the end of it. You will sit beside any child of seven to eleven and teach every bead move they will ever need. You will run a fifteen-minute session at home and a forty-five-minute class. And you will be able to tell a parent, in two minutes, why this is worth fifteen minutes of their child's day.

That last part is this lesson. Parents ask "why?" before they ask "how much?" So we start with why.

[on screen: Big promise · Story · One thing · Three secrets]

I'm going to show you a talk in four parts. Learn the order. You will give your own version of it to every parent you meet.

### Part one: the promise

The promise is simple. Fifteen minutes a day, a soroban and a chair beside the child. The child builds a picture of the beads in their head and learns to add, take away, multiply and divide on that picture.

That is all we promise. Speed and accuracy in arithmetic, built a day at a time. Hold that line. It is strong enough on its own.

### Part two: the story

Here is mine. You will tell yours.

I got C's in math. I had ADHD. I could not sit still, I forgot things, and I was always in trouble. Somebody decided early that I was not a math kid, and I believed them.

Then I wanted to play college sports. My GPA was a wall. I needed a test score nobody could argue with.

So I trained my brain the way I trained my body. Mental math and abacus drills, fifteen minutes a day. There was no kit. There was no parent book and no trainer. I pieced it together alone and guessed what came next.

Around week three, it clicked. I started to like it. My grades went up, and I was not studying more. I got a perfect ACT. I got a full ride. I was named Scholar-Athlete of the Year at a Division I school.

Same kid. Same ADHD. A different kind of smart.

[on screen: "Same kid. A different kind of smart."]

Now, a warning. That is my story, and it is true for me. It does not mean the beads treat ADHD. They do not, and you will never say they do. When you tell your story, tell what happened to you. Let the parent draw the line to their own child.

### Part three: the one thing

Everything we do pushes one belief. Here it is, word for word.

[on screen: Intelligence is built, not born.]

Intelligence is built, not born. And the mental abacus is the fastest way we know to build it at home, in fifteen minutes a day.

If a parent believes that, every other worry falls over. If they don't, no price is low enough.

### Part four: three secrets

A parent who says no is holding one of three wrong beliefs. Each one has an answer.

**Secret one is about the vehicle.** The parent thinks school will make their child sharp. School is built to move thirty kids at one pace. It rewards the kids who memorize. And the numbers are not good. In 2024, only 28 percent of US eighth graders were proficient in math. Scores were back where they were twenty-five years ago.

**Secret two is about the parent.** They think "my kid isn't a math kid," or "I'm not a math person." There is no such thing as a math kid. I was the proof. And the parent does not teach. They sit beside. You do the teaching, and the trainer picks the problems.

**Secret three is about time and money.** They think it takes years, or a tutor, or a center. A center is often $100 to $250 a month for one subject. A tutor is $40 to $80 an hour. Fifteen minutes a day at home beats two drives a week.

[on screen: Vehicle · You · Time and money]

### What the research says, and what it does not

Parents will ask if this is proven. Here is what you may say.

A three-year classroom trial in India followed 204 kids. They did about three hours a week. The abacus group beat the extra-math group on arithmetic. They also did better on place value. A study in China followed 144 kids for five years. They gained in arithmetic and in the visual memory that holds the picture.

Here is what those studies did not find. No IQ gain. No jump in general memory or attention. And a one-year trial with US first graders, two hours a week, found no benefit. Those kids could not yet read place value.

So we never promise IQ. We never promise memory or focus. We never say it works for every child. We never say results come in weeks. We never use words like treat or cure. And we never promise a parent, or you, any income.

What do you say instead? You say what the child will be able to do. "In a few months, your child can add five 2-digit numbers in their head." That is a promise the ladder keeps.

### Your homework

Below this video is a drill. It is a list of things parents hear. Sort them. Then write your own two-minute talk in the same four parts, with your own story in part two.

Your story does not need a scholarship in it. "My daughter froze on timed tests" is a story. "I was scared of math until I was forty" is a story. Tell the true one.

See you in Lesson 2, where we pick up the beads.

## Drill set

### A. Say it, or never say it

Sort each line. Write **say** or **never**. For every **never**, write a line you could say instead.

1. "Abacus kids score higher on IQ tests."
2. "In a trial in India, the abacus group beat the extra-math group on arithmetic."
3. "It works for every child."
4. "Your child will add five 2-digit numbers in their head by the end of the Ace stage."
5. "This helps kids with ADHD focus."
6. "Fifteen minutes a day at home beats two drives a week to a center."
7. "You'll see results in two weeks."
8. "Most instructors land between $20 and $40 a session. Your rates are yours to set."
9. "You'll make $2,000 a month teaching this."
10. "The five-year study in China found no change in IQ."
11. "It boosts memory and attention."
12. "There's no such thing as a math kid."

## Teach-back

Record a two-minute talk on your phone, as if a parent at school pickup asked "what is this abacus thing?" Use the four parts in order: promise, your story, the one thing, the three secrets. Then play it back and check it against the **never** list. One forbidden claim means you record it again.

Keep the recording. You will use it in Lesson 20.

5. Which three wrong beliefs do the three secrets answer?
