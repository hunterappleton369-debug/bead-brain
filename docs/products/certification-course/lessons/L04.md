# L04 · Two columns, left to right, and the first boss

Multi-digit problems run the way an abacus user runs them: set the first number, then add or take away each line, biggest place first.

## Video script

[HUNTER: over the shoulder. Show a printed four-line problem beside the soroban.]

So far we've worked on one rod. Today we use two, then three. And you'll see that bigger numbers are just more rods.

### The stacked problem

Here is a problem the way a child sees it on a page.

[on screen: a stacked problem: 32, + 51, − 13]

Three lines. Set the first number, 32. Tens rod 3, ones rod 2.

Now the second line, plus 51. Tens first. Three plus five on the tens rod. Is the heaven bead free? Yes. Bring it down. Then ones. Two plus one. Free earth bead? Yes. The beads show 83.

Third line, minus 13. Tens first. Eight take away one. Is there an earth bead on the beam? Yes. Pull it down. Ones. Three take away three. Pull three down. The beads show 70.

Read the answer off the beads. Seventy.

### The paper holds the problem, the beads hold the answer

Look at what we did not do. We did not write anything down in the middle. No running total on the page. The page holds the problem. The beads hold the answer. The child reads it at the end.

That is how every program in Japan runs it, and it's how the mental picture will work later. Nothing is written in between. The eyes stay one line ahead of the hand.

### Why left to right

School teaches adding right to left. Ones first, then carry. We go left to right, every time, from day one.

Here's why. Setting is left to right. Reading a number out loud is left to right. And when the child goes mental, the picture fills in from the big place down. A child who adds right to left on the beads has to flip the order in their head later. Don't make them flip it.

Right now, with free beads only, the order makes no difference to the answer. That's exactly why we lock in the habit now, while it's easy.

[on screen: "Left to right. Every time."]

### Three digits

Three-digit problems change nothing. Hundreds, then tens, then ones. Same moves. More rods.

### Spotting the school habit

You'll see it. The child's eyes jump to the ones column first. Say "left to right" and nothing else. Don't explain why. The why is for you, not for a seven-year-old.

### Writing your own problems

In Stage 1, the trainer and the printed sheets give you every problem. If you ever write your own for a class, check every line on your own beads first. Ask on each rod: are the beads free? If any rod needs a friend, change the number.

One problem with a hidden carry in Stage 1 teaches a child that the beads are unfair. They did everything right and still got stuck. Don't let that happen. Problems are picked for the lesson, always.

### The Sprout boss

Every stage ends with a boss test. It's timed. There are no hints. It has two to four short parts, and each part maps to one or two lessons. The child has to pass it to open the next stage.

Here's the Sprout boss.

[on screen: Sprout Boss · Read hundreds: 6 in 60 s, 5 right · Set hundreds: 5 in 90 s, 4 right · Direct add and take away: 6 problems, 3 lines, 2 digits, 4 min, 5 right]

Part one: read six three-digit numbers in under a minute. Five right to pass. Part two: set five three-digit numbers in ninety seconds. Four right. Part three: six problems, three lines, two digits, free beads only, four minutes. Five right.

Bosses are meant to take two or three tries. When a part fails, you know which lesson to go back to. Part one failed? Back to reading. Part three failed? Back to two columns. We go deeper on this in Lesson 13.

### How long Sprout takes

For a child of seven or older, about two to three weeks at fifteen minutes a day. Don't rush it. Sprout decides everything that comes after.

### What's next

Every problem today had free beads. Next lesson, we ask the question that every later move answers. What if the beads aren't free?
