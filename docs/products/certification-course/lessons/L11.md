# L11 · Drill types and tempo

The five kinds of drill every proven abacus program uses, what each one builds, and how fast to read numbers to a child.

**Week** 4 · **Video** <!-- runtime:start -->about 6 min<!-- runtime:end --> · **Lesson time** 90 min · **Mirrors** the trainer's drill kinds (read, set, written, dictation, flash, mental) and Stage 5 lessons "Flash anzan begins" and "Listening: numbers read aloud"

## Video script

[HUNTER: talking head, then short demos. A second person as the "child" helps for the dictation and flash demos.]

Walk into any good abacus school, in Japan, in India, in the US. You'll see the same handful of drills. The names change. The drills don't.

Today you learn all of them, what each one builds, and how fast to go.

### Why the big programs matter to you

The big programs have run these drills for a long time. UCMAS, for one, runs eleven levels in three modules: Foundation, Construction and Advanced. Each level takes five to six months. Kids come to a two-hour class once a week. Then they do fifteen to twenty minutes of homework a day.

[on screen: UCMAS · 3 modules · 11 levels · 2-hour weekly class · 15–20 min daily]

Look at the class format. Flash cards. Listening, which is dictation. Speed writing. And a graded exam at every level, with a bead paper and a mental paper.

Our stages map onto that. Sprout to Adept is roughly their Foundation. Ace and Wizard and Champion, their Construction. Prodigy to Legend, their Advanced and the dan grades. The big difference is the clock. They meet weekly, in groups. We go daily, fifteen minutes, one child at a time at home. Same content. More minutes with hands on the beads.

### Drill one: reading and setting

You know these from Lesson 2. See a number on the beads, say it. Hear a number, set it. They build the shapes. Every program starts here, and every good teacher comes back to it as a warm-up.

### Drill two: written sheets

A page of stacked problems. The child works each one on the beads, left to right, and says the move. The page holds the problem. The beads hold the answer.

Written sheets build the hand. They're where the thirty-four moves become automatic.

### Drill three: dictation

You read the numbers out loud. The child works them on the beads with nothing written down.

This is the oldest drill there is. Japanese schools run it by voice, with the whole class answering. It takes the child's eyes off the page and puts them on the beads, which is where they'll need to be later.

Start slow. One number every two seconds while the child is on the beads. Read each number once. Clean. Don't repeat it.

[on screen: Dictation: 1 number every 2 s on the beads]

### Drill four: mental

Soroban away. Eyes closed. You read the numbers. The child moves imaginary beads and says the total.

Let the fingers twitch on the table. Studies of mental-abacus users show that stopping the hands slows them down. The fingers aren't cheating. They're part of the picture.

Start at one number a second. That's slower than it sounds.

### Drill five: flash

Numbers appear one after another and vanish. The child keeps the total on the mental beads and says only the final answer.

A Japanese teacher, Yoji Miyamoto, built flash anzan to force kids off the physical abacus. You can't write. You can't look back. The numbers come too fast to say in words. So the picture has to do the work.

In the trainer, flash starts in Stage 5. Five one-digit numbers, each shown for a second and a half. At a table, you are the flash machine, and it can start sooner.

[on screen: Flash begins: 5 numbers × 1.5 s]

### How to read numbers

This matters more than it looks. Keep a steady beat. Say each number once. Say a minus as part of the number. "Minus eighteen," as one chunk. A child who hears "minus," then waits for the number, has already lost the beat.

The child says nothing until the end. Not the running total. Not the numbers back to you. Just the answer.

### When to speed up

Accuracy first, always. Stay at a tempo until the child is right nine times out of ten. Then go a little faster.

The mistake nearly every teacher makes is speeding up too soon. A child who is right half the time at a fast beat isn't building the picture. They're guessing. A child who is right nine of ten at a slow beat is building it. The speed will come.

[on screen: 9 of 10 right → then faster]

### When a child rushes

You'll see it. Seven out of ten, fast. The child has decided fast is the point. Say "slow is fine." Flat, as a fact. Then time nothing for a week. When accuracy is back at nine of ten, bring the clock back.

### Mixing them

A good session uses three of the five. A warm-up, usually reading. The lesson drill, usually written. And a short mental or flash round to close. We build that session in the next lesson.

### A. Written: 2-digit, five lines, small friends

On the soroban, from the page. Say the move, left to right.

1. 94 − 64 + 52 + 14 − 11
2. 65 + 12 − 36 + 11 + 26
3. 18 + 41 − 25 + 10 + 25

### B. Dictation: four 2-digit numbers

Read these aloud to a partner at one number every two seconds, or record yourself and play it back. The listener works on the soroban with nothing written down.

1. 15, 18, 57, 14
2. 10, 94, 80, 47
3. 38, 52, 63, 84
4. 17, 24, 21, 34
5. 56, 93, 73, 14

### C. Flash: five 1-digit numbers at 1.5 seconds

Say each number once on the beat. The listener says only the total.

1. 1, 7, 1, 8, 8
2. 5, 5, 2, 1, 9
3. 2, 2, 2, 3, 6
4. 6, 3, 2, 8, 3
5. 2, 3, 7, 9, 4

### D. Mental: three small numbers, free beads only

The first mental round a Stage 2 child gets. Practise the voice and the pause.

1. 2, 6, 1
2. 5, 1, 2
3. 3, 1, 5
4. 5, 2, 1
5. 5, 2, 2

## Teach-back

Record yourself reading sections B and C aloud, as if to a child. Use a metronome app if you have one: 30 beats a minute for dictation, 40 for the 1.5-second flash. Play it back. Mark every place you repeated a number, broke the beat, or said "minus" apart from its number. Record it again until there are none.
