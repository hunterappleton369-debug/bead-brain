# L17 · Anzan: building the picture

How the mental abacus grows from one rod to three or four, why it stops there, and what to do when it collapses.

**Week** 5 · **Video** <!-- runtime:start -->about 6 min<!-- runtime:end --> · **Lesson time** 90 min · **Mirrors** the mental lessons across the stages: Stage 1 "Eyes closed", Stage 2 "Mental: three small numbers", Stage 3 "Mental: crossing ten", Stage 4 "Mental: five single digits", "two 2-digit numbers" and "three 2-digit numbers", Stage 5 "Mental: five 2-digit numbers", Stage 7 "Mental: three 3-digit numbers", Stage 8 "Mental: ten 3-digit lines" and Stage 9 "Mental: 4-digit lines"

## Video script

[HUNTER: talking head, then a child (or adult) doing a mental round with eyes closed and fingers moving on the table.]

Everything so far has been on the wooden beads. Now we take them away.

Anzan is the Japanese word for mental calculation. In this method, it means doing the sum on a picture of the soroban in your head. The child sees the beads. Moves them. Reads the answer off the picture.

That picture is the goal of the whole program. The wooden soroban is how we build it.

### The picture is real

We know the picture is a real abacus, and no trick of counting, in three ways.

When trained users make a mistake, it's often off by exactly five. That's one heaven bead in the wrong place. Researchers found that pattern decades ago.

They can calculate while talking about something else. The words aren't where the math is happening. Studies found that talking doesn't break the picture.

And if you stop their hands, they slow down. The fingers are part of it. So when a child's fingers twitch on the table during a mental sum, let them. They're the engine. Most kids stop on their own as the picture gets stronger.

[on screen: Off by five · Words don't break it · Hands help]

### How big the picture gets

Here's the part that shapes how you teach. Research on mental-abacus users finds the picture holds about three or four rods at once. That's about the same as the number of objects anyone can track with their eyes.

[on screen: The picture holds about 3–4 rods]

So the picture grows in steps, and each step is one more rod.

Stage 1: one rod, eyes closed, for a second.

Stage 2: one rod, three small numbers, free beads.

Stage 3: one rod plus the rod beside it for the carry. Four one-digit numbers that cross ten.

Stage 4: two rods. Two two-digit numbers, then three.

Stage 5: two rods, five lines, plus and minus.

Stage 7: three rods. Three three-digit numbers. The edge of what most people can hold.

Stage 8: three rods, ten lines. That's the format of the Japanese 1st-kyu mental exam.

Stage 9: four rods. The trainer warns that this may take months to feel stable. It's near the limit.

That's why the ladder stops at three-digit flash and four-digit lines. Past that, the picture doesn't grow. Speed does.

### Why two-digit sums feel like a jump

When a child moves from one-digit to two-digit mental sums, it feels hard. Now they're holding two objects, each with its own beads. Expect a slow week. Don't make the rounds shorter to make it feel easier. Keep them the same size and slow the beat.

### Building it

The picture is built the same way at every stage. Real beads, then eyes, then head.

Do the sum on the real soroban. Watch the beads. Close your eyes. Replay it in your head. Then do the next one with no soroban.

That's also the fix when it breaks.

### When the picture collapses

The child says "I lost it" halfway through. Here's what happened. The picture turned into words. They caught themselves saying "sixty-two" in their head. Words are too slow.

The fix is always the same. Do that same sum once on the real soroban. Watch it. Close your eyes. Replay it. Then the next one in the head.

[on screen: "Back to the beads, then back to your eyes."]

That's the whole instruction. "Back to the beads, then back to your eyes."

### Keep the numbers inside the moves

The head can only use a move the hand already owns. So mental rounds must need only moves the child has learned on the beads.

In Stages 1 and 2, that means free beads only. If you make up a round, run each number on your own beads first. If it needs a move the child doesn't own, pick a different number. The trainer and the printed sheets do this for you.

### What kids say

You'll hear three things, usually in this order, and each one means the picture has grown.

"I can see it." The child is surprised. They mean it. A rod with beads, behind their eyes.

"My fingers did it." They noticed their hand moving, and that it helped.

"I don't know what the numbers were. I just know the answer." That's the picture working exactly right. Only the total was ever stored.

When a child says the third one, tell the parent. It's a big day.

### Running mental rounds

Say each number once, on a steady beat. Start at one a second. The child says nothing until the end. If a round falls apart, "Let it go. Next one."

Plateaus last about two weeks. They break overnight. Keep the daily mental drill the same size. Add dictation for variety. "Same as yesterday" is the line.

## Teach-back

Have a partner read you sections A to D while you work with eyes closed and fingers free. Then teach the "back to the beads, then back to your eyes" fix to a child or partner: give them a round from section C, and the first time they lose it, run the fix exactly. Write down what they said when the picture came back.
