# L18 · Flash anzan: tempos and the kyu table

The drill that convinces every grandparent: numbers that appear and vanish, and only the total stays.

**Week** 6 · **Video** <!-- runtime:start -->about 5 min<!-- runtime:end --> · **Lesson time** 90 min · **Mirrors** Stage 5 "Flash anzan begins" and "Flash: faster, longer", Stage 7 "Flash: 2-digit numbers", Stage 8 "Flash: 3-digit numbers" and "Flash: ten 3-digit numbers", Stage 9 and 10 flash lessons, and Playbook chapter 6

## Video script

[HUNTER: start with a flash round on screen, then talking head. If you can, show a child taking a five-number round at the table.]

Watch the screen.

[on screen: a flash round: five 1-digit numbers at 1.5 s each]

Five numbers. Each one appeared and vanished. Did you get the total?

That's flash anzan. It's the single most convincing thing a child will do with this method. And it costs nothing to run.

### Where it came from

A Japanese teacher named Yoji Miyamoto built flash anzan to push his students off the wooden abacus. Numbers flash one after another and disappear. You can't write anything. You can't look back. The numbers come too fast to say in words. So the only place the total can live is on the mental beads.

That's why flash beats a worksheet. On paper, a child can write the running total, glance back, count on fingers. In flash, there's nothing to glance back at. The picture grows because it has to.

### How far it goes

The Guinness record is fifteen three-digit numbers in 1.61 seconds, set by Rikako Miyamoto in February 2024. In June 2025, Takuya Yatani added thirty three-digit numbers in 3.30 seconds.

Ask record holders what the numbers were, and they can't tell you. Only the total was ever stored.

### The flash ladder in Japan

The Japan Flash Anzan Association grades from 20th kyu up to 20th dan. Each grade is ten questions, and seven right passes.

[on screen: 20th kyu: three 1-digit in 5 s · 12th kyu: ten 2-digit in 10 s · 1st kyu: fifteen 2-digit in 13 s · 2nd dan: four 3-digit in 4 s · 5th dan: ten 3-digit in 7 s · 10th dan: fifteen 3-digit in 3 s · 20th dan: 1.5 s]

The trainer's top stages are set against this ladder. Remember from Lesson 14: we say "about." We don't award their grades.

### Flash in the trainer

Flash starts in Stage 5. Five one-digit numbers at a second and a half each. Then eight numbers at one second. That's the Ace boss.

Stage 7: two-digit numbers, eight of them, at 0.7 seconds.

Stage 8: three-digit numbers. Five at one second, then ten at 0.8.

Stage 9: ten three-digit numbers at 0.7, then 0.5 seconds.

Stage 10: fifteen three-digit numbers at 0.4, 0.3 and 0.2 seconds. There's no top to that ladder.

### Flash at the table

You don't need a screen. You're the flash machine. And at a table it can start in Stage 2, with the numbers kept inside the moves the child owns.

[on screen: Level 1 · three 1-digit, free beads, adding · Stage 2]

Here's the first one. You say "Ready?" and wait for the nod. "Three." A beat. "Five." A beat. "One." The child says "nine." You say "yes," and nothing else. Next round.

Three rounds is a session. Three rounds a night is the whole game.

### Tempo

Keep a steady beat. One number a second is a good start. It's slower than it sounds. Say each number once, cleanly. Never repeat it. The point is that a number lands and is gone.

Stay at a tempo until the child is right nine times out of ten. Then go a little faster. At about 0.7 seconds a number, words can't keep up and the beads have to take over. That's why speed matters at all. Not for its own sake. Because speed forces the picture.

And the mistake nearly every teacher makes is speeding up too soon. A child right half the time at a fast beat is guessing.

### Two-digit rounds

Each two-digit number lands on two rods at once. "Thirty-four" is a three on the tens and a four on the ones, taken as one shape. A minus is part of the number. Say "minus eighteen" as one chunk.

### Breathing

Tell kids to breathe out as each number lands. That's the feeling to aim for. Calm. The number arrives, the beads move, it's gone.

And when a round falls apart, "Let it go. Next one." A lost round costs nothing. A child who learns to let one go can take the next number and still be right more often than you'd think.

### The grandparent show

First show: reading. Set a three-digit number, cover it after two seconds, the child says it. Week one.

Second show: eyes closed. Week two.

Third show: the flash round, properly, in Stage 5. Someone at the table will try to catch the child out. They'll go faster, or throw in a big number. Let them. One rule: one number at a time, said once.

Film it. Thirty seconds of a child taking five numbers is the clip every relative gets.

## Teach-back

Run three flash rounds from section A with a child (or an adult who has never done it), using the exact script: "Ready?", the nod, one number per beat, "yes" and nothing else. Then run section B with a partner reading to you. Use a metronome: 60 beats a minute for 1 second, 40 for 1.5 seconds. Write down your tempo, how many rounds were right, and whether you should speed up.
