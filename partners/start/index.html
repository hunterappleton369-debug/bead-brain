The only three things a video needs

Every video that sells does three things, in this order. Get these right and the lighting, the edit and the music stop mattering.

1 · Hook (the first 2 seconds)

Stop the thumb.

You have about a second and a half before a parent scrolls. The hook's only job is to buy you the next five seconds. Say it out loud and put it on screen as text. No "hey guys," no intro, no logo. Start mid-thought.

Four hooks that work for this offer:

  • Proof. Show a record kid first, talk second. "Watch this six-year-old. Each number flashes for a quarter of a second."
  • Contrast. "A math tutor near me is $200 a month. This is free."
  • Curiosity. "There's a reason every abacus-kid video gets millions of views."
  • Confession. "I was the parent who said 'we're not a math family.'" (only if it is true for you)

Twelve hooks ready to use (change the words to sound like you):

  1. "There's a reason every abacus kid video gets millions of views."
  2. "This six-year-old sees fifteen numbers for a quarter second each and adds them. Watch her hands."
  3. "A 14-year-old set six world records in one day. He started on this at age six."
  4. "A tutoring center quoted me a few hundred a month. I tried this first."
  5. "Private-school parents are quietly doing this at home."
  6. "If your kid is bored in math class, this is the opposite of a worksheet."
  7. "Kids in Japan still learn math on beads. Here's why that matters for yours."
  8. "Watch this kid race a calculator. Then I'll show you where it starts."
  9. "Stop telling your kid math is hard. Hand them this."
  10. "The one screen-free thing my kid asks to do after dinner." (true for you)
  11. "Nobody told me math could be a hands game."
  12. "The free thing I'd pick over a math tutor if I had to choose."
2 · Value (the middle 15 to 25 seconds)

One idea, shown.

Give the parent one thing they did not know. One. A video with three points sells nothing. Three value plays, pick one per video:

  • React to a proof clip. Play two or three seconds of a record kid (clips below), react like a person, then explain in one breath: "She's not guessing. She's moving an abacus she can see in her head. That's what this trains."
  • Explain how it works. The 20-second explainer in the next section. Hands moving beads on camera while you talk is the best version of this.
  • Your story. Your kid's first night on the beads. The price you were quoted for tutoring. What you wish you had at eight. True stories only; they sound different and parents can tell.

Kids on the beads are the best footage there is. Hands, clicks, a face that is concentrating. Your child can be in every video; you do the talking about the offer and the code.

Tell them exactly what to do.

Say your code twice. Put it on screen. Say the link slowly. Then pin a comment with both.

How the abacus works (so you can explain it in 20 seconds)

Most kids meet numbers as squiggles. Seven is a shape you memorize. On an abacus, seven is something you can hold: one bead worth five pushed down to the bar, two beads worth one pushed up. The number has a weight, a position, a feel under your finger.

The kit's abacus is the Japanese soroban. Every rod has one bead on top worth five and four below worth one each. Ones on the right, then tens, then hundreds, marching left. Adding is moving beads. Carrying is a flick to the next rod. There are no times tables to memorize at the start. The fingers do the thinking, and the hands teach the head.

Here is the part that looks like magic. After enough practice, the child stops needing the beads. Ask for 348 plus 276 and they go quiet, their fingers twitch in the air, and the answer comes out. They are moving an abacus they can see in their head. The Japanese call it anzan, mental calculation. The kids in the clips who add fifteen flashing numbers in under two seconds are doing exactly this, at speed, after years of practice.

Why it sticks. The brain stores the picture, not the words. Brain scans of trained abacus kids show number work lighting up the visual and spatial parts of the brain, the same circuits you use to catch a ball or picture your kitchen with your eyes closed, instead of the language circuits most of us grind through math with. That is what people mean by "whole-brain" math: both hands, both eyes, the picture side of the brain working with the word side.

Why kids like it. It is a game with a score. Beads click. You see the answer. Fifteen minutes a night, and the first time your kid beats you to an answer, you will both be hooked.

"An abacus turns numbers into something your kid can touch. Add with your fingers long enough and your brain starts doing it with a picture instead of words. That's how those kids add faster than a calculator."
"It's the Japanese soroban. Beads worth one and five on every rod. Kids learn to move them with their fingers, then with their eyes closed. The beads end up in their head."
"Every one of those record-breaking math kids you've seen online started on this exact tool. Not an app. Beads."

What you can say

Go big on the benefits abacus training is known for, show the record kids as what is possible, and stay honest about timelines and results. That is the whole rule.

Big benefits you can claim

  • Sharper focus and concentration (sitting still for 15 minutes of beads is itself the training).
  • Stronger memory and visualization: the child learns to picture and move the beads in their head.
  • Speed and accuracy with numbers, mental math without a calculator.
  • Confidence: the kid who answers first stops being scared of math.
  • Whole-brain development: hands, eyes and the picture side of the brain working together, not just rote memory.
  • The skills IQ tests measure, like working memory, visualization and pattern recognition. Other abacus programs say "improves IQ"; you can say abacus training builds those skills. Do not put a number on it ("15 IQ points").
  • "The training behind the world's fastest human calculators and the record-setting kids."

Proof you can show and cite (in plain words)

  • The Guinness record for adding 15 three-digit numbers flashed on a screen is 1.61 seconds, set in 2024 by a Japanese abacus competitor.
  • A 14-year-old set six Guinness records in one day in 2025. He started on the abacus at six and practices for hours a day.
  • In a three-year study of 204 kids aged 5 to 7, the children trained on the mental abacus pulled ahead of their classmates in arithmetic, and the classmates were getting extra math time too.
  • Brain scans show trained abacus kids use the visual and spatial parts of the brain for numbers.
  • About a million children in Japan learn the abacus (a Guardian estimate), and it is still taught in Japanese schools.
  • The clips below: a teen racing a calculator on live TV and winning, six-year-olds adding flashed numbers on Little Big Shots, a girl adding 100 three-digit numbers read aloud in 45 seconds.

Keep it honest (short list)

  • Do not promise a specific result by a specific date ("two grade levels by Christmas," "results in a week").
  • Do not invent a Child Phenoms customer story, and do not call the kids in the clips "Child Phenoms students." They trained elsewhere, for years.
  • No medical claims: nothing about ADHD, dyslexia, autism or "curing" anything.
  • The only price facts: the kit is free, shipping is $6.95, no subscription. No "only 50 left" or "ends tonight."
  • When you show the record kids, add a line like "those kids trained for years; results vary." On screen or spoken.
  • Your own experience is the strongest thing you have. Tell it straight.

Where to post, and how to link

Anywhere parents watch you. Parents order in one place, childphenoms.com, and your link just takes them there with your code filled in.

TikTok

Your link in the bio (TikTok turns it on at 1,000 followers; under that, say the code and "childphenoms dot com" and type it in your pinned comment). Say the code in the video, show it on screen, pin a comment with the code and link spelled out. Switch on the commercial content toggle when you post.

Instagram

Link in bio. Reels with the code on screen. Stories with the link sticker pointing at your link, which converts well because the tap is one step. Add "Paid partnership" if the label is available to you, or #ad in the caption.

YouTube Shorts

The same vertical video. Your link in the description and in a pinned comment (links are tappable there). Say the code anyway.

Facebook

Your page or profile, and parent, homeschool or private-school groups where the admins allow it. Post as a person with a story, not an ad. Never paste the link into a group that bans promotion.

Pinterest, newsletters, blogs

A pin or a paragraph with your link works for months. Pinterest parents search "math for kids" every day.

Not allowed

Coupon or deal sites, paid ads that use the Child Phenoms name, bulk messages, buying engagement, posting your code in other people's comments.
